# Empowering Video Translation using Multimodal Large Language Models 

## Bingzheng Qu, Kehai Chen*, Xuefeng Bai, and Min Zhang 

Abstract —Recent developments in video translation have further enhanced cross-lingual access to video content, with multimodal large language models (MLLMs) playing an increasingly important supporting role. With strong multimodal understanding, reasoning, and generation capabilities, MLLMs-based video translation systems are overcoming the limitations of traditional cascaded pipelines that separately handle automatic speech recognition, machine translation, text-to-speech and lip synchronization. These MLLM-powered approaches not only achieve competitive or superior translation quality, but also demonstrate stronger robustness in zero-shot settings and multi-speaker scenarios, while jointly modeling semantic fidelity, timing, speaker identity, and emotional consistency. However, despite the rapid progress of MLLMs and extensive surveys on general video–language understanding, a focused and systematic review of how MLLMs empower video translation tasks is still lacking. To fill this gap, we provide the first comprehensive overview of MLLMs-based video translation, organized around a three-role taxonomy: 1) Semantic Reasoner, which characterizes how MLLMs perform video understanding, temporal reasoning, and multimodal fusion; 2) Expressive Performer, which analyzes LLM-driven and LLM-augmented techniques for expressive, controllable speech generation; and 3) Visual Synthesizer, which examines different types of video generators for high-fidelity lip-sync and visual alignment. Finally, we discuss open challenges in video understanding, temporal modeling, and multimodal alignment, and outline promising future research directions for MLLMs-powered video translation. 

Index Terms —Video Translation, Multimodal Large Language Models, Video Understanding, Text-to-Speech, Video Generation. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

# VIDEO translation has become a critical research domain for addressing language barriers in global video communication. Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) present a new paradigm for this challenge. Real-world applications such as multilingual video conferences require not only accurate cross-lingual translation, but also faithful preservation of the speaker voice characteristics, precise lip synchronization, and coherent emotional expressiveness consistent with the original video. This imposes stricter requirements than conventional text-only or speech-only translation, as systems must jointly preserve semantic fidelity together with speaker identity, temporal alignment, and emotional expressiveness across modalities. II. PRELIMINARIES 

A. Video Translation 

We briefly review four core tasks in video translation: ASR, MT, TTS, and lip synchronization. 

1) Automatic Speech Recognition: Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) [150], [151], also referred to as speech-to-text transcription [152], [153], converts speech signals into textual token sequences. Modern ASR is mainly built on end-to-end architectures, including CTC [154], attention-based encoder–decoder models [153], [155], RNN-Transducers [156], and large-scale weakly supervised pretrain-ing [59]. In video translation, ASR serves as the front-end module that generates time-aligned transcripts for downstream MT, TTS, and lip synchronization; thus, recognition errors can propagate through the pipeline and degrade semantic fidelity and temporal alignment. 

2) Machine Translation: Machine Translation (MT) [157]– [160] transforms source-language transcripts into fluent target-language text. The field has evolved from statistical methods to neural sequence-to-sequence frameworks [159], [161], and later to Transformer-based and multilingual NMT systems [160], [162]. In video translation, MT operates on ASR transcripts and produces linguistically fluent and temporally suitable text for subtitles, and subsequent speech and visual generation. 

3) Text-to-Speech: Text-to-Speech (TTS) [163]–[166] con-verts text into natural and intelligible speech waveforms. Early systems were dominated by concatenative and parametric methods, whereas modern TTS mainly relies on neural architectures such as autoregressive vocoders [165], sequence-to-sequence models [166], [167], and non-autoregressive or flow-based generators. In video translation, TTS synthesizes target-language speech from MT output and must maintain not only naturalness and intelligibility, but also timing, rhythm, and expressive consistency with the source video. 

4) Lip Synchronization: Lip synchronization [133] is the final stage of the video translation pipeline, where upstream decisions from ASR, MT, and TTS become visually exposed. It aligns translated speech with mouth motion and facial dynamics by mapping target-language speech to appropriate lip movements. Earlier approaches relied on rule-based phoneme-viseme mappings [168], whereas recent neural methods learn this alignment directly from data through audio-driven facial reenactment and talking-head synthesis [169]. Effective lip-sync must preserve temporal coherence, prosodic consistency, and natural facial appearance across languages. 

B. Multimodal Large Language Models 

MLLMs [183], [184] extend large language models with visual and audio inputs, enabling reasoning across modalities. Unlike earlier multimodal systems based on shallow fusion, recent MLLMs adopt LLM-centered architectures in which modality adapters map video and audio features into token representations compatible with the language model [175]. Representative directions include contrastive and caption-based vision-language pretraining [185], video-language modeling for long-term temporal reasoning [186], and audio language modeling based on Whisper-like or speech-oriented frameworks [187]. III. A MULTI-ROLE TAXONOMY FOR MLLMS-BASED VIDEO TRANSLATION 

We organize MLLMs-based video translation into three functional roles: Semantic Reasoner, Expressive Performer, and Visual Synthesizer. This role-oriented taxonomy highlights how MLLMs reshape video translation from modular pipelines to multimodal reasoning and generation as shown in Fig. 1. 

A. The Semantic Reasoner 

As the comprehension core of video translation, the Se-mantic Reasoner converts multimodal evidence into reasoning-ready representations [215], [216]. Recent progress has been driven by advances in long-form understanding, multimodal alignment, temporal reasoning, and efficient representation learning [25]–[27], [51]–[53], [64], [65], [217], and can be summarized along three closely related aspects: video-language alignment, spatio-temporal modeling, and training paradigms. First, alignment methods map spatiotemporal visual features into the token space of the LLM through projection modules such as linear layers, Q-Former blocks, or cross-attention, with representative designs including Video-ChatGPT [3], Video-LLaMA [4], LLaMA-VID [6], Valley [7], ChatBridge [18], and EVE [17], [66]. Second, to strengthen temporal reasoning without incurring the full cost of model adaptation, many methods adopt parameter-efficient tuning, as exemplified by LLaMA-Adapter [218] and BT-Adapter [20], which improve motion-aware understanding and multimodal fusion with lightweight temporal modules. Third, semantic reasoning is also shaped by training strategy and data construction: Otter [24] emphasizes in-context instruction tuning, InternVideo2 [23] adopts a progressive multimodal training pipeline, RED-VILLM [22] reuses pretrained image-MLLMs with temporal adaptation, and Video-LLaMA 3 [21] transfers image-text pretraining to video tasks. B. The Expressive Performer 

The Expressive Performer is responsible for generating target speech that is intelligible, temporally aligned, and stylistically consistent with the source video. Existing methods can be broadly grouped into LLM-driven TTS , where the language model directly participates in speech generation, and 

LLM-augmented TTS , where it serves as an auxiliary module for controllability, representation learning, or adaptation. The former emphasizes zero-shot synthesis, promptability, and prosody control, as illustrated by CosyVoice [68], Spark-TTS [75], HALL-E [73], and MegaTTS 2 [67]; the latter places greater emphasis on generation fidelity, robustness, and controllable adaptation, with representative examples including VALL-E R [90], XTTS [83], NaturalSpeech 3 [95], F5-TTS [80], and ControlSpeech [92]. Overall, these two lines reflect a broader shift from conventional TTS toward zero-shot, instruction-aware, and controllable speech generation for dubbing-oriented video translation, which we provide the summary of models in speech synthesis tasks in Table II. 

C. The Visual Synthesizer 

The Visual Synthesizer is responsible for generating lip movements and facial dynamics that are temporally aligned with translated speech. Recent approaches are mainly based on diffusion-driven video generation and talking-head synthesis, and can be broadly divided into UNet-based and DiT-based architectures. UNet-based models remain practical backbones because they preserve spatial detail well and inherit strong priors from text-to-image diffusion models, making them effective for lip synchronization and controllable facial editing; representative examples include MagicVideo [121], Tune-A-Video [128], and ControlNet-based designs [219]. By contrast, DiT-based models [220] are increasingly attractive for long-horizon generation because they model long-range spatial and temporal dependencies more effectively; for example, Vidu [140] improves efficient latent-space long-video generation, while Phantom [141] enhances theme-consistent generation through reference-guided multimodal diffusion. Overall, the main trade-off is that UNet-based models offer stronger practicality and controllability, whereas DiT-based models provide better temporal coherence and scalability for video translation. The current multimodal large models have made significant breakthroughs on this basis, both in terms of benchmarks and the models themselves [54], [221]. 

IV. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 

Despite recent progress, MLLMs-based video translation still faces a fundamental gap between multimodal understanding and translation-quality requirements. Current models remain limited in both fine-grained understanding [173], [222] and long-form video comprehension [31], [37], [51]: they often miss subtle cues such as facial expressions [223] and object interactions [224], while also struggling to preserve temporal coherence and contextual continuity over long videos [31], [37]. Temporal modeling is likewise insufficiently unified, since existing methods rely on either explicit cues or implicit mechanisms such as relative position embeddings [225], [226] and sequence-level context learning [227], yet still fail to jointly support fine-grained alignment and long-range narrative consistency; this calls for temporal representations that encode local event structure [228] together with long-term dependencies [229]. In addition, multimodal alignment remains weak in realistic settings because raw audio cues are underused, synchronization across video frames, audio, ASR, and translated text is difficult [230], large-scale audio–visual–text corpora are scarce [231], and multimodal modeling remains computationally expensive [232]. Taken together, these limitations suggest that future research should move toward hierarchical and memory-augmented video understanding, adaptive frame selection [233], stronger temporal attention and retrieval-augmented reasoning [234], [235], tightly coupled audio–visual–text modeling with high temporal precision [236], self-supervised multimodal pretraining [237], stronger pretrained audio models such as Whisper-like architectures [238], and efficient deployment strategies based on structured sparsity and streaming inference [239], [240]. 

V. Conclusion 

In this survey, we present a systematic review of the emerging roles of MLLMs in video translation. We first revisit conventional video translation pipelines and we propose a restructured task paradigm that places MLLMs at the core of the pipeline and recasts video translation around three functional roles of MLLMs to clarify their potential in semantic understanding, translation execution, and multimodal generation as Reasoner, Performer, and Synthesizer. To support ongoing research, we provide a structured overview of widely used datasets, benchmark and metric comparisons, high lighting key performance trends across different roles of the MLLMs in video translation task. Finally, we discuss open challenges and future research directions from three key angles: video understanding, temporal modeling and multimodal alignment across visual, acoustic, and linguistic streams. We hope this survey offers a coherent conceptual framework and a roadmap for advancing the integration of MLLMs into next-generation video translation systems. 7
