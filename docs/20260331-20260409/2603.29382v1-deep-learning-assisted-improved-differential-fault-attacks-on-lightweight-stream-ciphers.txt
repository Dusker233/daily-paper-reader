# Deep Learning-Assisted Improved Differential Fault Attacks on Lightweight Stream Ciphers 

Kok Ping Lim, Dongyang Jia, Iftekhar Salam 

School of Computing and Data Science, Xiamen University Malaysia, Sepang 43900, Malaysia 

{CYS2209667, CME2209122, iftekhar.salam }@xmu.edu.my 

Abstract. Lightweight cryptographic primitives are widely deployed in resource-constraint environment, particularly in the Internet of Things (IoT) devices. Due to their public accessibility, these devices are vulner-able to physical attacks, especially fault attacks. Recently, deep learn-ing–based cryptanalytic techniques have demonstrated promising results; however, their application to fault attacks remains limited, particularly for stream ciphers. In this work, we investigate the feasibility of deep learning assisted differential fault attack on three lightweight stream ci-phers, namely ACORNv3, MORUSv2 and ATOM, under a relaxed fault model, where a single-bit bit-flipping fault is injected at an unknown loca-tion. We train multilayer perceptron (MLP) models to identify the fault locations. Experimental results show that the trained models achieve high identification accuracies of 0.999880, 0.999231 and 0.823568 for ACORNv3, MORUSv2 and ATOM, respectively, and outperform tra-ditional signature-based methods. For the secret recovery process, we introduce a threshold-based method to optimize the number of fault in-jections required to recover the secret information. The results show that the initial state of ACORN can be recovered with 21 to 34 faults; while MORUS requires 213 to 248 faults, with at most 6 bits of guessing. Both attacks reduce the attack complexity compared to existing works. For ATOM, the results show that it possesses a higher security margin, as majority of state bits in the Non-linear Feedback Shift Register (NFSR) can only be recovered under a precise control model. To the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first experimental results of differential fault attacks on ATOM.

# 1 Introduction 

Over the past two decades, various cryptographic competitions, such as eS-TREAM, the Competition for Authenticated Encryption: Security, Applicability, and Robustness (CAESAR) and National Institute of Standards and Technol-ogy (NIST) Lightweight Cryptography Project were called for the design and third-party analysis of lightweight cryptography primitives. Lightweight cryp-tography is designed to ensure secure communication for resource-constrained 

> arXiv:2603.29382v1 [cs.CR] 31 Mar 2026

devices, e.g., Internet of Things (IoT) devices, as traditional ciphers are intended to operate in resource-rich environments. Many cryptanalysis techniques, such as cube attack, side channel attack and fault attack are applied to the lightweight ciphers. Among these attacks, fault attacks have gained significant attention in recent years due to their effectiveness against physical hardware and the ongo-ing advancement of fault injection techniques. Since IoT devices are commonly deployed in publicly accessible areas, adversaries often have physical access to them, making these devices viable targets for fault attacks [23]. Fault attack was first introduced by Boneh et al. [4] and targets physical hardware rather than the cryptographic algorithm itself. They applied the attack to RSA and Robin signature schemes by introducing hardware faults to factor the large prime modulus N . In the same year, Biham and Shamir [3] implemented a variant of fault attack, differential fault attack (DFA), and applied it against the Data Encryption Standard (DES) to recover the 56-bit secret key. The attack works by inducing an error (fault) during the operation of the cipher to obtain the faulty outputs. By analysing the differences between fault-free and faulty outputs, some secret information of the cipher, such as the internal state or secret key can be recovered. Hoch and Shamir [8] further extended DFA against RC4, LILI-128 and SOBER-t32, introducing DFA on stream ciphers. Following these foundational works, differential fault attacks were further extended and refined, and subsequently applied to evaluate the security of a wide range of cryptographic primitives. Orumiehchiha et al. [15] applied the first DFA against WG-8 stream cipher to recover the initial state and secret key of the cipher using 6 faults with the time complexity of 2 24 . Ma, Tian and Qi [11] applied DFA against LIZARD stream cipher. Using 6 faults, the initial state of LIZARD can be recovered. Salam et al. [20] performed bit-flipping, probabilistic random and deterministic random fault attacks on Grain128AEAD to recover the initial state. Salam et al. [21] managed to recover the initial state and secret key of CLX-128 by applying bit-flipping and random fault attack, with 54 and 134 faults, respectively. Rostami et al. [18] applied a random fault attack against Enocoro-80 and Enocoro-128v2. The result shows that the attack only required 2 faults to recover the internal state of Enocoro-80 and Enocoro-128v2 with the time complexity of 2 16 and 2 29 , respectively. Radheshwar and Roy [17] applied DFA agaisnt ChaosForge stream cipher and managed to recover the internal state and the secret key. Prajasantosa and Salam [16] were able to recover 58 state bits and 15 key bits of TinyJAMBU with bit-flipping and random fault attacks, under different control models. Recently, some works utilized deep learning methods to assist cryptanalysis on various lightweight block ciphers, such as finding the distinguishers of block ciphers [7,9,27]. The deep learning approach has also been used to predict the se-cret key of lightweight block ciphers, such as S-DES and S-AES [10]. In terms of deep learning assisted fault attack, Baksi et al. [1] developed multilayer percep-tron (MLP) models to identify the fault location for lightweight stream ciphers, Grain128a and Lizard, which achieves better performance than the traditional signature-based method. Cheng et al. [5] proposed a deep learning-based fault analysis, using MLP and Convolution Neural Network (CNN) to recover the key of AES with 1 fault and an average of 1488 ciphertexts. Saha et al. [19] implemented a fault attack leakage assessment test with deep learning to detect fault-induced leakage in the ciphertext distributions. Mondal et al. [14] devel-oped a deep neural network for identifying the fault location of Subterranean 2.0 and recovered the embedded secret key with no more than 5 faults. The existing literature shows that differential fault attacks remain a popular cryptanalytic technique for state recovery or key recovery. In addition, the inte-gration of deep learning in fault attack provides a new direction in enhancing the performance of the attack. Given the limited research on deep learning–assisted DFA, the feasibility of such fault attacks remains largely underexplored. To ad-dress this, we propose a deep learning–assisted DFA framework for enhancing differential fault attacks and apply it to ACORNv3, MORUSv2, and ATOM. 

1.1 Existing Differential Fault Attacks on ACORN and MORUS 

Dalai and Roy [6] implemented differential fault attacks on the first and second version of ACORN. By precisely injecting 326 faults and guessing 120 state bits, the full internal state of cipher at the 39-th clocking of encryption phase can be recovered, with an attack complexity of 2 120 . Zhang et al. [28] applied DFA against ACORNv2 under the fault model of no control over the fault location. By analyzing the occurrence of 1 in the differential keystream, the fault location were identified with a high probability of 97.08%. For equations solving, they used linearization and guess-and-determine strategies to solve the equations and recover the initial state. The linearization strategy required 31 to 88 faults to recover the initial state with an attack complexity of c · 2179 .19 −1.76 n, where n

was the number of fault injection, and c was the time complexity of solving the equations. For the guess-and-determine method, guessing 67 state bits could recover the initial state of the cipher with 41 fault experiments. Siddhanti et al. [22] extended DFA to ACORNv3, optimizing the classical differential framework by incorporating both forward and backward state-update equations and fixing selected bits before SAT solving. In their attack, the fault locations were identified using the signature-based method. Due to the large state size of ACORN, the attack was optimized by guessing 20 state bits. Their work showed that the initial state of ACORNv3 can be recovered using 9 faults and 1200-bit keystream, with the time complexity of 2 25 .40 . Zhang et al. [29] also performed DFA on ACORNv3, following the same attack process as in [28]. To identify the fault location, they introduced the “unique” versus “non-unique” differential sets method. By incorporating the keystream extension and high probability priority strategies, the accuracy of their proposed method was 99.998%, using 163-bit differential keystream. For the equations solving, a guess-and-determine method was used to recover the full initial state of the cipher. The attack complexity is c · 2146 .5−3.52 n, where n is the number of faults such that 26 < n < 43 faults and where c was the time complexity of solving the equations. In short, most of the existing works of DFA against ACORN requires the guess-and-determine method to recover the state. In addition, some of the works focus on retrieving only linear equations, that require more fault injections to recover the state of the cipher. Additionally, the attacks based on fault model with no control over the fault location rely on traditional signature-based method to identify fault location. Hence, this presents a gap for investigating the use of deep learning for identifying the fault locations and improving the attack complexity. Furthermore, investigation can be conducted by incorporating high degree equations with a threshold-based technique to optimize the number of faults. Wong et al. [24] performed a theoretical analysis of DFA on MORUS, target-ing the bit-wise AND operation of the keystream generation function, where a random fault is injected into one of the two involved registers. With these faults, an adversary can reveal the non-zero state bits in the targeted register when-ever the differential keystream bits are 1. Repeated fault injections allow these non-zero bits to be recovered with high probability, while the remaining bits are assumed to be 0 with high confidence. However, the attack only achieved partial state recovery and and its reliance on precise control over the fault location is a relatively strong assumption; leaving open the the possibility of achieving full state recovery of MORUS with a weaker assumption.

# 3 Attack Description 

Our attacks are conducted in a simulation-based environment, where the fault injection is simulated by code, rather than implementing the attack on a physical device. The attack assumes a known-plaintext attack (KPA) model, where the adversary has access to specific keystream bits. Besides, the following assump-tions are made: 

– The adversary can inject a single-bit bit-flipping fault at the initial state 

– The adversary has no control over the fault location. 

– The adversary can always reset the cipher and run the cipher with the em-bedded key and initialization vector (IV) to observe the keystream. The attack consists of two phases, the fault location identification and secret recovery process. The details of these two phases are discussed in the following section. 

3.1 Fault Location Identification 

The fault location identification is the critical process in our attack, as the fault is injected to unknown location. Without the knowledge of fault location, we cannot obtain the valid differential keystream equations to recover the secret of the cipher. Thus, MLP models are trained to identify the fault location using differential keystream datasets. 

Dataset Generation Since there is no public dataset for fault location identi-fication for each cipher, we generate the datasets by ourselves. The procedure of dataset generation for each cipher is defined in Algorithm 4. For each possible fault location f of the cipher, 1536 samples are generated, each with a random key, K and IV, V . The key-IV pair is used to run the cipher for producing the fault-free keystream Z. After that, the cipher is reset and run with the same key-IV pair. At this time, a bit-flipping fault is injected to location f in the ini-tial state of the cipher. The faulty initial state is then used to produce the faulty keystream Z′. The differential keystream is then computed by XOR-ing Z with 

Z′. Finally, the key-IV pair, fault-free keystream, faulty keystream, differential keystream and the fault location are recorded into the csv file. Algorithm 4 Dataset Generation

For each cipher, three datasets are generated, one for training, one for evalu-ation and one for validation. To enhance the neural network model in capturing the pattern of fault propagation, the train-test ratio is set as 80:20. The size of training dataset is set as n · 210 , while testing and validation datasets are n · 28,where n is the number of possible fault locations. Hence, each fault location has 210 samples for training, 2 8 samples for testing. Note that the validation dataset is used to evaluate the performance of the model during the training. The length of the differential keystream is set to the range between 50% and 60% of the state size of the cipher, as referred to the work of Baksi et al [1]. 

Implementation of Multilayer Perceptron Model Using the generated datasets, we employ multilayer perceptron (MLP) models to identify the fault locations. The implementation of the models are done with the Keras library provided in the TensorFlow framework, using the Google Colab. The number of neurons in the input layer is the length of the differential keystream, while the number of neurons in the output layer is the size of the internal state of the cipher, that is the number of possible fault location. For the number of hidden layers, number of neurons or other hyperparameters, an exhaustive search is used to optimize the parameters. The model is compiled with the Adam optimizer and the sparse categorial cross entropy loss function. Since this is a classification problem, accuracy is chosen as the metric. The model is trained for a maximum of 50 epochs and the early-stopping criterion based on validation accuracy is applied. If there is no im-provement in validation accuracy for seven consecutive epochs, the training pro-cess is stopped, and the weights of the best validation accuracy are restored. 3.2 Secret Recovery 

Once the MLP models are trained, we proceed to apply the differential fault attack on the targeted ciphers. Figure 1 illustrates the process of secret recovery method. The attack starts by obtaining the fault-free keystream of the cipher. Once the fault-free keystream is obtained, the cipher is reset and run to the initial state. At this stage, a single-bit bit-flipping fault is injected to the initial state of cipher, where the fault location is unknown.We then run the cipher with the faulty initial state and obtain the faulty keystream. The computed differential keystream is then fed into the trained model to identify the fault location. When the fault location is correctly identified, we proceed to the next stage of equations construction. In contrast, the cipher is reset and repeat with the previous process when there is an error in fault location identification. With the correct fault location, we are able to generate the correspond-ing faulty keystream equations. By XOR-ing the fault-free and faulty keystream equations, the respective differential keystream equations are obtained. A thresh-old is introduced to determine the minimum number of differential equations for optimizing the number of fault injections. When the number of differential equa-tions is less than the defined threshold, we repeat the fault injection process.Note that the value of threshold varies across ciphers; this will be discussed in Sec-tion 4. If the number of differential equations has exceeded the threshold, we pro-ceed to solve the equations with Gr¨ obner basis provided in SageMath library. Meanwhile, we set a timer of one minute to determine if Gr¨ obner basis can solve the equations system. If Gr¨ obner basis returns the result within the time, we then verify the recovered secret bits. Otherwise, the current computation is ter-minated, while the threshold is increased by 5 and the process of fault injection and equations construction are repeated. Increasing the threshold allows more differential equations to be added into the equations system, providing more information to the Gr¨ obner basis for solving the equations system. 

# 4 Implementation and Results of Attacks

4.1 Differential Fault Attack on ACORNv3 

For the attack on ACORN, the adversary is assumed to have knowledge of 152-bit keystream. Before the attack, the multilayer perceptron (MLP) model is imple-mented to facilitate the process of fault location identification. In the attack, the adversary obtains the differential keystream and feeds it into the trained model to identify the fault location and generate the corresponding differential equations with respect to the initial state of the cipher. Once a sufficient number of differential equations is collected, those equations are solved to recover the initial state of ACORNv3.

# 5 Conclusion 

This work focuses on investigating the feasibility of deep learning assisted dif-ferential fault attack against ACORNv3, MORUSv2 and ATOM with the fault model of single-bit bit flipping fault with no control over the fault location. We have implemented three MLP models for identifying the fault location on ACORNv3, MORUSv2 and ATOM, where the accuracies of the trained models have improved the traditional signature-based method. Besides, we propose a threshold-based strategy to optimize the number of fault injections. The results show that we can recover the initial state of ACORN with the complexity of negligible and achieve full state recovery on MORUS, that are better than the results of existing works. On the other hand, we have implemented the first DFA on ATOM and managed to recover majority of state bits in NFSR under the fault model of precise control over the fault location. It shows that the design of double key filter has improved the security margin of the cipher. Future works may examine the feasibility of deep learning assisted fault attack under the more relaxed fault attack model of random fault with no control over the fault loca-tion. Besides, one may consider to remove the timer used in equation solving, to examine the number of faults needed to recover the state. Furthermore, one can proceed to solve the equation that encounter error in fault location identification, as it is expected that some of the state bits can be recovered correctly. References
