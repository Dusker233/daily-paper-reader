# SoK: Colluding Adversaries in Machine Learning Pipelines 

## Vasisht Duddu 1, Lipeng He 1, Asim Waheed 1, N. Asokan 1,21University of Waterloo, 2KTH Royal Institute of Technology {vasisht.duddu, lipeng.he, asim.waheed}@uwaterloo.ca, asokan@acm.org 

## Abstract 

Machine learning (ML) models are susceptible to various se-curity, privacy, and fairness risks. Adversaries with different characteristics (i.e., objectives, knowledge, and capabilities) 

can collude by executing one attack to amplify others. Exist-ing work lacks a systematic framework to explore collusion among adversaries, and to study the implications of the ad-versaries’ characteristics. We present a framework covering collusion (a) between train- and inference-time adversaries, and (b) among inference-time adversaries 1. Our framework accounts for factors enabling collusion between adversaries. We propose a guideline to conjecture about the potential for collusion using enabling factors. We use it to explain prior work, conjecture about unexplored collusions, and empirically validate five such cases. Finally, we discuss how adversaries’ characteristics influence the potential for collusion. 

## 1 Introduction 

Machine learning (ML) models are susceptible to a wide range of risks to security [23, 32, 75, 80, 96], privacy [84, 86], and fairness [69]. Prior work explored attacks to exploit vul-nerabilities associated with each of these risks. However, ad-versaries with different characteristics (i.e., objectives, knowl-edge, and capabilities) in the ML pipeline, either with different roles (e.g., model trainer and client) or with the same role (e.g., clients with different objectives), can collude . Collusion is the explicit coordination among adversaries to execute one attack and use the outcomes to improve another (e.g., [101]). Collu-sion among adversaries with different objectives is well doc-umented in other domains: cryptocurrency pump-and-dump schemes where one adversary inflates a stock, which can be exploited by others for profit [38]; and cyber-crime markets where an adversary sells compromised data for downstream attacks like identity theft [47]. Similar collusion within ML pipelines is feasible, and merits investigation. For instance, consider two different adversaries: Adv 1 seek-ing to find an adversarial example against a target model M

and Adv 2 seeking to build a shadow model of M either for some privacy attack [60], or to steal M ’s functionality [76]. 

Adv 1 sends multiple queries to M until it successfully finds an adversarial example [4], usually discarding all other query-response pairs. But, they are valuable for Adv 2. Therefore, 

Adv 1 can either sell the query-response data to Adv 2 or share the cost of querying M . Hence, adversaries are incentivized to collude (a) via a marketplace for selling attack information, or (b) by sharing costs to avoid duplication of efforts. Foreseeing all possible cases of collusion is challenging. A unified framework can help practitioners and researchers to identify new threats, design strong attacks to audit models, and design defenses against them. Ideally, the framework should be (i) comprehensive (captures an adversaries’ characteristics 

across multiple risks ) and (ii) extensible (allows inclusion of additional risks and characteristics). We present the first such framework that includes various factors aiding the potential for collusion. Our framework cov-ers two collusion types identified from prior work: (i) between train- and test-time adversaries ( → “train-test collusion ”), and (ii) among test-time adversaries ( → “test-time collusion ”). For both, we observe that a successful attack changes some fac-tors as an outcome, which can influence the effectiveness of other attacks relying on those factors. However, these factors differ between both collusion types. For train–test collusion ,poisoning changes overfitting and memorization which can impact the effectiveness of test-time attacks that rely on those factors. Here, the framework includes the factors related to overfitting and memorization, that can be manipulated by an adversary. For test-time collusion , knowledge inferred from the first attack is an outcome that satisfies the prerequisites of other attacks. Here, the framework includes factors related to the adversary’s knowledge. Using this framework, we propose a guideline for conjec-turing about collusion potential by identifying factors that are shared across attacks. We empirically validate our con-jectures for five unexplored cases of collusion. Finally, we explore how an adversary’s characteristics impact collusion potential and discuss implications for our guideline. We claim 1

## 2 Background and Related Work 

Notations: An ML model ( Mθ) is a function parameterized by θ that takes an input x (e.g., images, tabular data, graphs, or text prompts) and produces Mθ(x). For classification tasks, 

Mθ(x) is a classification label, while for generative tasks, 

Mθ(x) is an image or text. Hereafter, we denote Mθ by simply writing M . We assume training data ( Dtr ) follows certain distributional properties (e.g., a specific female-to-male ratio). We train M by updating θ to minimize the loss between 

M (x) and the expected output y. After training, we evaluate the utility on a test dataset ( Dte ), where we use accuracy for classifiers, and the quality of generated images (e.g., using Fréchet Inception Distance) and text (e.g., using perplexity) for generative models. We refer to predictions, intermediate activations, θ, and gradients as model observables .

Attacks against ML Models: Following the taxonomy in Duddu et al. [29], we consider an adversary ( Adv ) executes an attack by exploiting vulnerabilities corresponding to security, privacy, and fairness risks: • Evasion ( Evsn ) modifies the inputs to force M to misclas-sify (classifiers) [80,96], or generate unacceptable images/text (generative models) [82, 119]. In classifiers, these modified inputs are called adversarial examples ( xadv = x + δ) obtained by adding perturbations δ to x where δ is computed to maxi-mize M ’s loss. Similarly, in generative models, we generate adversarial prompts but to evade safeguards against unaccept-able content (or jailbreaking). • Poisoning ( Poisn ) trains M using malicious data records (poisons ) injected in Dtr to degrade M ’s utility on Dte [23]. A variant of poisoning is backdoors ( Bkdr ) where M is trained with poisons embedded with a specific pattern (aka trigger ), which maps an input to an Adv -chosen output. Backdoored inputs will generate Adv -chosen output, but clean inputs will result in normal behavior [2, 57, 128]. We refer to both of them as Poisn for brevity. • Unauthorized Model Ownership ( ModExt ) occurs when 

Adv obtains either an identical copy of M or derives a surrogate model Msur from M using model extraction at-tacks [14, 53, 76, 99]. We focus on the latter setting. • Membership Inference ( MemInf ) identifies if an input was in Dtr , by exploiting the difference in M ’s behavior on data records inside and outside Dtr [27, 28, 84]. • Attribute Inference ( AttInf ) identifies the value of an input’s sensitive attribute, by exploiting the difference in M ’s behavior on inputs with different attribute values [49]. In generative models, this translates to extracting personally identifiable information [62]. • Data reconstruction ( DtRecon ) recovers inputs or data records from Dtr using access to M [41, 114, 124, 131]. For generative models, M can generate outputs which either repli-cate or closely resemble confidential Dtr [3, 10, 13]. • Distribution Inference ( DistInf ) identifies the distri-butional properties of Dtr by exploiting the difference in 

M ’s behavior when trained with different distribution val-ues [70, 94, 95, 103, 130]. For instance, it differentiates be-tween M trained on Dtr with a male-to-female ratio of 0.1vs. M trained on Dtr with a ratio of 0 .9. • Discriminatory Behavior ( Discr )2 is observed when M

behaves differently across demographic subgroups identified by attributes such as race or gender on Dte [40, 61, 69]. This is measured using the difference in accuracy or false posi-tive/negative rates across different subgroups. There are different attacks corresponding to each risk but for brevity, we use the notations for risks as attacks and catego-rize them as train-time (i.e., Poisn ) and test-time (i.e., Evsn ,

MemInf , AttInf , DistInf , DtRecon , ModExt , and Discr ). 

Comparison to Prior Surveys/SoKs: Several surveys and SoKs study individual risks to ML models in isolation, in-cluding evasion [80, 96], unfairness [69], privacy [84], back-doors [2], poisoning [23], and unauthorized model owner-ship [75]. Salem et al. [86] relate multiple privacy risks using game-based formulations, though without consider-ing collusion among adversaries. A separate line of work explores trade-offs among defenses and risks, where de-fenses designed for one risk may inadvertently amplify oth-ers [29, 36, 71, 93, 116]. Other works have explored trade-offs among defenses such as fairness, interpretability, privacy, and robustness across diverse settings [18, 35, 42], and explore how to combine defenses without conflict to mitigate mul-tiple risks simultaneously [30, 97]. Prior works either focus on systematizing individual risks or trade-offs induced by the defender’s choices. This includes unintended interactions of defenses with risks (e.g., [29, 71]) or conflicting interac-tions among defenses (e.g., [30, 97]). None of the prior works systematize how adversaries may collude with each other.

## 3.1 Factors Aiding Collusion Potential 

For illustration, we consider collusion among two adversaries (Adv 1 and Adv 2), and discuss extending beyond two adver-saries in §7. Based on prior work (§4), we identify two collu-sion types and discuss the factors aiding their potential: • Train-test Collusion: Adv 1 can specifically optimize the train-time attacks, to increase the effectiveness of a test-time attack (as Att 2) executed by Adv 2. • Test-time Collusion: Adv 1 first executes a test-time at-tack (as Att 1), and uses the knowledge inferred to amplify another test-time attack (as Att 2) executed by Adv 2. 

Train-Test Collusion: We conjecture that train-time attacks, (Poisn ) can be optimized to influence various factors underly-ing overfitting and memorization as an outcome (e.g., increase tail length of Dtr ’s distribution, distinguishability in model observables across subgroups or datasets). This can impact the effectiveness of test-time attacks that rely on those factors. Concretely, Adv 1 can optimize Poisn to increase memoriza-tion and thereby the distinguishability between (a) training and non-training data records (to increase effectiveness of 

MemInf [101]), (b) across subgroups (to increase effectiveness of AttInf [101]), or (c) across datasets with different distribu-tions (to increase effectiveness of DistInf [16]). Hence, our framework includes factors which influence overfitting and memorization that can be manipulated by Adv 1, and exploited during test-time by Adv 2. We use factors influencing overfitting and memorization identified by Duddu et al. [29] who explore an orthogonal problem of how defenses impact susceptibility to unrelated risks. From these, we select factors that Adv 1 can control by optimizing Poisn , and exclude hyperparameter-related ones 3

(e.g., number of attributes, Dtr size, model capacity) and those which are insufficiently explored (e.g., priority of learning stable attributes, distance to decision boundary). There is a potential for collusion if there exist a pair of attacks where the outcomes from Att 1 can meet the prerequisites of Att 2. ## 3.3 Guideline to Explain/Predict Collusion 

We conjecture that the potential for collusion can be predicted by analyzing the common factors between the two attacks, i.e., comparing “How attack effectiveness leads to change in factors” and under “How changes in factors correlate with attack effectiveness” in Tables 1 and 2. However, defining an exact algorithm is challenging due to the complex and only partially understood dynamics of various factors. For instance, “poisoning/backdoors increases < Att 2 >” in case of train-test collusions, and “ Att 1 increases Att 2” for test-time collu-sions. We then reviewed the citations of these initial papers for comprehensive coverage. Overall, we collected 28 and 12 papers for train-test and test-time collusion respectively.

## 7 Discussion and Summary 

Framework and Requirements: We evaluate our frame-work against the two stated requirements: comprehensiveness and extensibility. 

R1 Comprehensive: Our framework is comprehensive with respect to both the risks considered for collusion and the factors used to relate them. • Comprehensiveness of risks: Our framework covers security, privacy, and fairness risks that are sufficiently studied in prior work to support systematic reasoning about collusion across the ML pipeline. These include both train-time and test-time risks for which interactions can be characterized through shared underlying factors. Risks that lack sufficient prior analysis to enable such char-acterization are outside the scope of this work. For example: 

– Side-channel attacks [7, 48, 113] operate under a black-box M (BBox ) where a Clnt or SrvProv exploits physi-cal or system-level leakage to extract model architecture, and partial model parameters. 

– Fault injection [65, 83, 115] occurs when a Clnt or 

SrvProv exploits hardware vulnerabilities to induce tar-geted faults during execution or weight storage, enabling accuracy degradation or targeted misclassification. 

– Adversarial initialization [44, 45] is a train-time attack where a ModProv or ModTrnr initialize the model such that standard training on benign data converges to Adv -desired behavior (e.g., backdoors, increased training time, or biased outputs). 

– Data ordering attacks [51,88] is a specific form of Poisn 

in which a DtOwnr or DtProv poisons the model by re-ordering data during training. 

– Sponge examples [24,89,106] violates availability where 

Clnt sends carefully crafted inputs that increase energy consumption and latency. As research on these risks matures, they can be incorporated into our framework by reasoning about Adv ’s characteris-tics and any additional factors required for the risk. • Comprehensiveness of factors: Our framework identifies a set of train-test and test-time factors that underlie collusion among risks. These factors are drawn from prior work and are common across the risks considered in this paper (§2). We show how each risk relates to all identified factors, thus enabling systematic reasoning about collusion (Tables 1 and 2). While we do not claim that the identified factors are complete, our framework allows adding more factors. Overall, our framework is comprehensive within its defined scope of risks in §2 (meets R1 ) and can be extended to incor-porate new risks and factors. 

R2 Extensible: Our framework is designed to support ad-ditional factors, risks, and adversary characteristics. To add new factors, we can specify how each factor relates to exist-ing risks, as illustrated in Tables 1 and 2. For new risks, we characterize how the risk relates to the existing factors (e.g., Tables 1 and 2 and §3.2). Once these relations are defined, the guideline can be used to study the potential collusion. Overall, our framework satisfies the extensibility requirement ( R2 ). 

Takeaway: Our proposed framework is comprehensive (R1 ) and extensible ( R2 ). 

Extending Beyond Two Adversaries: Our framework ex-tends naturally to collusions with more than two adversaries. We first discuss two cases where Adv 1 colludes with multiple adversaries in parallel (we use “ ⇒” for “colludes with”): • Train Adv 1 ⇒ Multiple Test Adv s

Adv 1 can optimize train-time risks to increase suscepti-bility to multiple test-time risks, enabling collusion with several test-time adversaries. • Test Adv 1 ⇒ Multiple Test Adv s

Adv 1 executes a test-time attack and uses the knowledge learned as a prerequisite to increase the effectiveness of other test-time attacks. This will allow Adv 1 to collude with multiple test-time adversaries. For the above two cases, we can apply our guideline (from §3.3) between Adv 1 and each of the other test-time adver-saries in parallel. The potential for overall collusion will be determined independently by the potential for each pairwise collusion. We now present a case for sequential collusion :• Test or Train Adv 1 ⇒ Test Adv 2 ⇒ Test Adv 3 ⇒ · · · 

Adv 1 colludes with Adv 2 to increase effectiveness of Att 2

executed by Adv 2, whose success provides knowledge en-abling downstream test-time collusion. Here, we can apply our guideline sequentially to each pair-wise collusion. The potential for overall collusion will be determined by the potential for pairwise collusions. More complex combinations of the parallel and sequential cases 12 may exist, but our guideline can be readily extended to predict potential collusion. 

Adaptive Strategies: One possible setting involves multi-round collusion, where Adv 1 iteratively adapts their attack strategy based on the success of Adv 2’s attack in the previ-ous round. In this scenario, the effectiveness and collusion potential are expected to increase across rounds. Specifically, improvements in Att 1 by Adv 1 based on feedback from pre-vious round, will further improve the effectiveness of Att 2

executed by Adv 2. This iterative amplification is captured by our guideline through an increase in correlation between 

Att 1 and the factors across successive rounds, consequently explaining the increase in effectiveness of Att 2.

Exceptions to Guideline: We do not claim our framework is perfect; rather, it is a first step toward systematically un-derstanding and predicting the potential for collusion among adversaries. Our guideline cannot assess collusion when there are no common factors. While we did not encounter any such cases, further work would be needed to either uncover more relations between risks and factors to apply the guideline, or identify additional missing factors that can enable the collu-sion. This can then allow us to apply our guideline to evaluate collusion among adversaries. 

Application of Framework and Guideline: Various stake-holders in the ML pipeline can be impacted by the increased effectiveness of colluding adversaries. For instance, data own-ers , model owners , and service providers become more sus-ceptible to security, privacy, and fairness risks under collusion. Our framework helps identify possible sources of collusion, the enabling factors, and the potential impact of such collu-sion. ML security researchers and practitioners can use our framework to study previously unexplored collusion settings, develop stronger attacks for auditing, design defenses, and analyze enabling factors. Practitioners can further account for these factors to reduce collusion potential (see below), and use our guideline to efficiently assess collusion risks. 

Potential Mitigations: By explicitly identifying the enabling factors through our framework and guideline, our work can help researchers and practitioners design defenses that disrupt collusion. For train-test collusion, defenses such as detecting and filtering poisoned samples in Dtr [92], modifying train-ing objectives to reduce the influence of poisons [127], can reduce the impact of Poisn that enable subsequent attacks. Additionally, specific factors can be explicitly targeted to re-duce correlations (e.g., reduce subgroup distinguishability using fairness constraints [1]). For test-time collusion, defend-ers can apply various transformations or filters on inputs and outputs to restrict the information that Adv can infer through interactions with M . More broadly, combining complemen-tary defenses that mitigate different risks can further reduce collusion potential [30]. 

Limitations and Future Work: We identify the following limitations and directions for future work: • Our framework focuses on correlations rather than causa-tion, since the limited interpretability of model behavior and the poor understanding of factor–attack dynamics make it difficult to causally identify enabling factors. As a result, our framework is conceptual and the proposed guideline is heuristic in nature, similar to Duddu et al. [29]. Developing a principled guideline with causal analysis is an important direction for future work. • Our evaluation empirically validates the heuristic guideline without providing theoretical guarantees. We evaluate the framework by (a) demonstrating that it explains collusion in prior work and (b) empirically validating it on five illustra-tive case studies. However, the evaluation remains limited in establishing the general effectiveness of the guideline. Providing theoretical justification for different collusion settings and their corresponding conditions is an important future research direction. • The scope of attacks explored in this work is limited to well-studied security, privacy, and fairness risks with sufficient prior literature to establish correlations and validate the guideline. Additional attacks are possible, as discussed in §7 under “Framework and Requirements: R1 Comprehensive”. However, further empirical analysis is required to establish the corresponding correlations and apply our guideline. • Although not empirically evaluated, we analyzed prior work spanning different model types (e.g., generative models), settings (e.g., federated learning and retrieval-augmented generation), and modalities (e.g., text, images, and tabular data). This suggests that the identified factors may general-ize across diverse models and settings. However, our work does not provide a comprehensive evaluation of such gen-eralization, which we leave for future work. Additionally, other generative-model-specific factors, currently excluded due to unexplored attack correlations, can be naturally in-corporated into the framework. 

Summary: Existing work lacks a systematic framework for exploring colluding adversaries in ML pipelines. We intro-duce the first framework by identifying factors which enable collusions. We propose a guideline to identify potential collu-sion using the factors, explain prior work, conjecture about unexplored ones, and empirically validate five such cases. We then study how adversaries’ characteristics influence the po-tential for collusion, based on which we update our guideline. 

## Ethical Consideration 

We discuss ethical considerations based on impact to various stakeholders, risks to ethics during the research and publi-cation process, various mitigations to reduce ethical risks, justification for conducting research and publishing. 

Impacts to Stakeholders: The primary stakeholders im-pacted by this work are: data subjects whose susceptibility to risks (e.g., to privacy or fairness) may worsen with colluding adversaries; model owners and service providers who face increased security and privacy failures when threats are evalu-ated as interactions among risks instead of isolated risks; ML 

13 security researchers and practitioners who may adopt our framework for auditing and designing defenses; and society 

as colluding adversaries can undermine trust in ML systems used in high-stakes domains. 

Risks and Benefits of Research: We do not collect new data, involve human subjects, or target real-world systems. All empirical results validate conjectures using standard datasets and models from prior work. As such, the research process itself poses minimal direct risk to data subjects or institutions. By explicitly characterizing train–test collusion (where training-time manipulation increases susceptibility to inference-time attacks) and test-time collusion (where out-comes of one inference attack satisfy the prerequisites of another), our framework may lower the barrier for adversaries to identify effective attack sequences. However, the primary ethical benefit is defensive: our anal-ysis reveals that evaluating risks in isolation can substantially underestimate real-world harm. In particular, our systemati-zation shows that mitigations targeting a single risk may fail when adversaries collude across stages of the ML pipeline. This can help model designers account for such risks and design more effective defenses. 

Mitigations: We do the following to limit potential harm: • Using Existing Attacks: Our framework relies on previ-ously published risks and attacks and show how existing attacks can be amplified under collusion. • Defensive Framing: Collusions are presented as evaluation blind spots, intended to inform auditing and defense rather than to maximize adversarial effectiveness. • Scoped Empirical Validation: Experiments are limited to validating conjectured relationships between risks, not to demonstrating real-world exploitability. There is still risk that adversaries could use our framework for attack design, but this is an inherent trade-off in our work. 

Decision to Conduct and Publish: We justify publishing this work under the principles of beneficence and public inter-est . Colluding adversaries already exist in practice; failing to make these interactions explicit leaves defenders unprepared and disproportionately harms data subjects. Therefore, we conclude that the benefits of publication outweigh the risks.

## Appendix A Streamlined Threat Model 

We represent an adversary model as TM which is a tuple of sets representing different characteristics of Adv . To define the threat model, we identify various Adv ’s characteristics: objectives, capabilities, knowledge, and access to M and Dtr .Formally, we represent the threat model as a tuple of seven sets (denoted as <.>), corresponding to the questions below: (i) what role(s) does Adv play? ( Role ) (ii) what are Adv ’s objectives? ( Obj ) (iii) what are Adv ’s capabilities? ( Cap )(iv) what is Adv ’s interaction with M ? ( I) (v) what attack optimizations can Adv use? ( Opt ) (vi) what is Adv ’s knowl-edge about M ? ( KM); and (vii) what is Adv ’s knowledge about data? ( KD) Given these characteristics, we denote a threat model TM describing Adv as a tuple of the seven sets:

## B Evaluating Streamlined Threat Model 

Comprehensive: We show that our threat model can repre-sent prior work covering different risks, model types, settings. We show this in our open-sourced artifact where we specify 

Adv ’s characteristics covering individual risks and collusions from the literature survey 9. We survey some papers to cover a variety of model types and settings, to illustrate that our streamlined threat model is comprehensive. • To cover different model types, we searched for the follow-ing keywords: language models, audio models (automated speech recognition), graph models, image classifiers, vision language models, reinforcement learning, diffusion models, text-to-image models, generative adversarial networks, and variational autoencoders .• To cover different settings, we searched for the following keywords: MLaaS, transfer learning, online learning, fed-erated learning, and vertical federated learning .• We selected papers from top tier security and privacy (e.g., USENIX, NDSS, S&P, and CCS), machine learning (e.g., ICML, ICLR, NeurIPS, AAAI), and computer vision (e.g., CVPR, ICCV, ECCV) venues. • Overall, we collected 95 papers for Evsn , 53 for 

Poisn /Bkdr , 29 for ModExt , 70 for MemInf , 32 for AttInf ,13 for DistInf , and 74 for DtRecon .

Extensible: To add a new role for Adv , we update Role and specify Adv ’s characteristics ( Cap , Opt , I, KM and KD). For new risks, we update objective set ( Obj ) to define who can perform the attack, describe the optimizations ( Opt ), type of interaction ( I), and the required knowledge ( KM, KD).
