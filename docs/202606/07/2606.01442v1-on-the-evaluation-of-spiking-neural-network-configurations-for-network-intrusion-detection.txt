# On the Evaluation of Spiking Neural Network Configurations for Network Intrusion Detection 

Raj Patel ∗, David Amebley †, Taye Akinrele ‡, Shaswata Mitra §, Sayanton Dibbo ∗∗¶ , Shahram Rahimi ∥

Department of Computer Science, The University of Alabama {rpatel38 ∗, dkamebley †, toakinrele ‡, smitra3 §, sdibbo ¶, srahimi1 ∥}@ua.edu 

Abstract —Network intrusion detection is a core component of modern cybersecurity infrastructure, yet the deep learning models that dominate the field are computationally demanding, motivating interest in lightweight alternatives suited to edge and neuromorphic deployment. Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) are therefore a natural candidate, but their design space, spanning the choice of neuron model and spike encoding scheme, remains poorly characterized for intrusion detection. We bridge this gap by using a controlled ablation study using 9 neurons coupled with 3 spike encoding schemes, making 27 variants, all implemented on snntorch evaluated over raw inputs with limited preprocessing on four benchmark datasets (NSL KDD, KDDCup99, CIC-IDS2017, and CTU-13) with 5 seeds. We find that spike encoding scheme is a better determinant for detection quality than the neuron model, where rate and delta spike encodings perform worse than latency encoding over the sweep. The LeakyParallel neuron with latency encoding performed the best overall, averaging at 92 .11% accuracy and 0.80 macro-F1 at a rate of 2.01% false positives averaged over all 4datasets, with accuracy close to perfect for CIC-IDS2017 and CTU-13, and also performed the fastest on inference. These results highlight the potential of SNNs as a viable alternative to traditional methods of intrusion detection when considering low-latency or resource-constrained deployments. 

Index Terms —Spiking Neural Networks, Cyberattacks, Deep Learning, Network Intrusion Detection, Cybersecurity. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The rapid proliferation of the Internet of Things (IoT) [1], now embedded in Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) across nearly every critical sector, has sharply enlarged the global at-tack surface [2], [3]. The active IoT installed base is projected to reach 27 billion devices by 2025 [4], and these networks now generate an estimated 79 zettabytes of traffic annually [5]. Because CPS tightly couples computation with physical processes, intrusions threaten not only data confidentiality but also the operational integrity and physical safety of critical infrastructure. Robust, real-time Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) at the edge are therefore urgent. State-of-the-art intrusion detection relies predominantly on Deep Neural Networks (DNNs), which achieve strong accu-racy through learned feature representations [6] but require continuous, synchronous processing and dense matrix multi-plications that impose significant computational and memory overhead. This conflicts with the resource-constrained, low-power nature of IoT edge nodes [7], and offloading inference to the cloud introduces latency, bandwidth saturation, and 

** Corresponding author. 

delayed threat response incompatible with real-time security [8]. Most DNN-based NIDS are also benchmarked on static, offline datasets rather than live traffic, limiting their adapt-ability to real-world edge conditions. Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs), the third generation of neural networks, provide a very attractive alternative. They are asynchronous and event-driven and process dis-crete temporal spikes instead of continuous activations [9]. Since computation is only performed on spike events, they significantly reduce energy consumption and are well-suited for neuromorphic and low-power edge hardware [10]. Their temporal coding represents information in the timing of spikes, not in scalar magnitudes [11]. It naturally matches the bursty nature of network traffic, e.g. inter-packet timing, flow duration and protocol sequencing. This property makes SNNs an affordable, yet believable candidate for detecting live-edge traffic in real-time, where DNNs are costly. Recent work has begun applying SNNs to network secu-rity. Zhou and Li [12] showed that nonleaky, single-spike temporal-coded neurons ease gradient-based training and, on NSL-KDD and AWID datasets, outperform conventional machine learning and DNN baselines, giving early evidence of competitive SNN detection. Wang et al. [6] later developed a convolutional SNN with strong precision and reduced energy consumption. SNNs have shown further promise in encrypted traffic classification [13] and energy-efficient edge intrusion detection [14]. Each of these works relies on a predetermined single neuron model and encoding scheme. As a result, the SNN design space for intrusion detection remains fragmented: there is no common framework for comparing how the input encoding and neuron dynamics jointly shape detection performance. SNN-based intrusion detection is still underexplored com-pared to the broader, vision-dominated SNN literature [6], and it is still unclear whether SNNs can process high-dimensional tabular traffic at scale while remaining competitive [15]. Most importantly, each previous study has been committed to a single configuration and the joint effect of the neuron model and encoding scheme on detection quality and computational cost has never been systematically characterized, and thus, practitioners have no deployment guidance [16]. To address this, we present the first systematic study, jointly varying the neuron model and spike-encoding strategy across multiple intrusion detection benchmarks with a single fixed training pipeline, isolating each design choice.

Section II describes the datasets, threat model, and experimental methodology, Section III presents the results, and Section IV concludes. II. M ETHODOLOGY 

We perform a controlled ablation study to dissect the two most direct design choices that shape a spiking neural network (SNN) intrusion detector: the neuron model and the 

spike-encoding strategy . Since spike activations are binary and sparse, SNN inference replaces the dense multiply-accumulate operations of conventional DNNs with simpler accumulate events, the basis for the energy savings reported on neuromorphic hardware [10], [17]. We therefore measure inference cost in addition to detection quality. This section operationalizes contributions C1 and C2 (Sec-tion I): it describes the fixed pipeline that holds all other fac-tors constant (C1) and the metric suite used to evaluate each variant (C2). To keep the comparison meaningful, every other pipeline component, including data preprocessing, network topology, optimizer, training budget, and evaluation protocol, is held fixed across all configurations. Fig. 1 summarises it as five modular stages, of which only Stage 3 (encoding) and the neuron model within Stage 4 is varied. 

A. Threat Model 

We consider a network intrusion detector deployed at a monitoring point, such as an edge gateway, which observes traffic as a stream of flows and must separate benign activity from malicious traffic. Detection operates on per-flow features and on per-source-host behavioral aggregates for CTU-13, rather than on packet payloads, keeping it applicable as traffic becomes increasingly encrypted. The threats in scope are the attack categories present in our datasets: denial-of-service (DoS), reconnaissance and probing (Probe), remote-to-local access (R2L), and user-to-root privilege escalation (U2R) under the KDD five-class taxonomy, together with botnet command-and-control traffic in the binary CTU-13 setting. We assume the availability of labeled historical traffic for supervised training, deployment distribution similar to the training distribution and malicious flows with statistically dis-cernible feature patterns, the premise behind learning-based detection. The detector issues alerts (normal vs attack) instead of mitigation. Adversarial evasion and data poisoning, open-set detection of attack categories not in the training taxonomy, and live stream operations are out of scope, revisited as future work. These boundaries are used to keep the study in the context of the effect of the neuron model and the encoding choices on the detection quality and cost in a standard supervised IDS setting. B. Datasets and Preprocessing 

We evaluate on four widely used network intrusion de-tection datasets: NSL-KDD [18], KDDCup99 [19], CIC-IDS2017 [20], and CTU-13 [21]. The first three are treated as multi-class problems under the canonical KDD five-class taxonomy {Normal, DoS, Probe, R2L, U2R}, while CTU-13 is a binary botnet-detection problem {Normal, Botnet}. Table I reports the post-preprocessing input dimensionality, classification setting, and split sizes, and Table I gives the per-class sample counts. We pre-process all datasets in the same manner to better capture the impacts only by the neuron and encoding choices. Categorical features like protocol, service, and flag are one-hot encoded against the union of all splits, ensuring consis-tent feature dimensionality between train and test. Heavy-tailed numeric fields (byte counts, packet counts, durations) are passed through a log(1 + x) transform to compress their dynamic range, after which all features are min-max scaled to [0 , 1] ; this scaling is a prerequisite for the spike encoders, which interpret the feature magnitudes as spike intensities. Non-feature columns such as timestamps and IP addresses are removed. For CTU-13 where the public per-flow representation is too coarse for reliable botnet detection, each flow is further enriched with per source-host behav-ioral statistics (connection counts, unique destination and unique-port counts, port and protocol entropies) following the host-aggregation principle established in the previous botnet-detection work. We evaluate on the official training and testing splits of NSL-KDD and KDDCup99. CIC-IDS2017 and CTU-13 do not provide a canonical split, we adopt a stratified 70/30 train and test partition. In all cases, the training portion is further divided 90/10 into training and validation sets, with the latter used only for per-epoch monitoring. We purposely maintain the original class distribution of each dataset rather than use synthetic oversampling; the extreme rarity of the R2L and U2R classes (Table I) is instead surfaced through imbalance-robust metrics (Section II-E).

C. Spike Encoding Strategies 

Each preprocessed feature vector x ∈ [0 , 1] d is converted to a binary spike train S ∈ { 0, 1}T ×d over T discrete time steps. We compare three encodings that span the dominant paradigms in the neuromorphic literature [22]:  

> •

Rate coding: each feature drives a Bernoulli (Poisson-like) spike source whose firing probability equals its magnitude, St,i ∼ Bernoulli( xi), so that information is carried by spike frequency.  

> •

Latency coding: each feature emits a single spike whose timing is inversely proportional to its magnitude, ti =

⌊(1 − xi)( T − 1) ⌋, so that stronger features fire earlier and information is carried by spike timing, producing sparse trains.  

> •

Delta modulation: a spike is emitted whenever the temporal contrast of a feature exceeds a fixed threshold, capturing change rather than absolute level. 

D. Neuron Models and Network Architecture 

We exclude biophysically detailed models such as the Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) neuron, which is defined by four coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations (ODEs) per neuron and is computationally expensive to simu-late, making it impractical for large-scale training and resource-constrained edge deployment. We instead evalu-ate nine lightweight models from the snntorch library [22], grouped by the dynamics they add to the basic spik-ing mechanism: first-order leaky integrate-and-fire ( Leaky ,

Lapicque , LeakyParallel ), conductance-based second-order models ( Synaptic , Alpha ), recurrent variants that feed output spikes back as input ( RLeaky , RSynaptic ), and gated sequence cells ( SLSTM , SConv2dLSTM ). Using the canonical Leaky neuron as an illustrative case, the membrane potential Ui of neuron i evolves as 

Ui[t + 1] = β U i[t] + X

> j

Wij Sj [t] − θ S i[t], (1) where β is the membrane decay and θ the firing threshold; a spike Si[t] = Θ( Ui[t] − θ) is emitted when the potential reaches θ (Θ is the Heaviside step), and the reset term subtracts θ on spiking. The remaining models retain this threshold-firing rule but replace the update with their respec-tive conductance, recurrent, or gated dynamics. All variants share a common feed-forward topology: a linear projection maps the d-dimensional input to a hidden spiking layer, whose output spikes feed a second linear projection into a leaky output layer with one neuron per class. The network is unrolled over the encoding window, and the per-class output spikes accumulated across time steps serve as logits. The LeakyParallel variant vectorizes this unrolling, removing the per-step recurrence over time. Hidden width, time steps, firing thresholds, and the remaining hyperparameters are held fixed across all variants and reported in Section II-F. 

E. Evaluation Metrics 

Because several datasets are severely imbalanced (Table I), we report a broad metric suite rather than accuracy alone. For classification quality we use accuracy, balanced accu-racy, macro-F1 and weighted-F1, macro precision and recall, weighted one-versus-rest ROC-AUC, and the Matthews corre-lation coefficient (MCC). The latter two are robust to class im-balance and cannot be inflated by a majority-class predictor. For the intrusion-detection setting, we additionally collapse predictions to a binary normal-versus-attack view and report the Detection Rate (DR) and False Alarm Rate (FAR). Finally, to characterize inference cost we record latency, defined as the mean wall-clock milliseconds per sample and measured with explicit device synchronization so that the timing reflects compute rather than queue depth. All metrics are computed by a single shared module for consistency across the 540 runs. F. Experimental Setup 

All experiments are implemented in PyTorch 2.12 with 

snntorch 0.9.4 and scikit-learn 1.8.0, and executed on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU under Python 3.12. To isolate the effect of the neuron and encoding choices, we vary only the neuron model, drawn from nine snntorch families, and the spike encoding, one of three schemes (rate, latency, and delta); all other settings, including the architecture, optimizer, and training budget, are held fixed, and no per-variant hyperparameter tuning is performed. The network is a two-layer spiking multilayer perceptron with hidden width 

H=128 , unrolled for T =25 time steps. Spiking neurons use a membrane decay of β=0 .85 , and the conductance-based families ( Synaptic , Alpha ) additionally use a synaptic decay of α=0 .90 . Non-differentiable spike emission is han-dled during backpropagation through time by the arctangent (ATan) surrogate gradient, the snntorch default. The two gated sequence neurons ( SLSTM and SConv2dLSTM ) use a reduced firing threshold of 0.1, which we found necessary to prevent spike quiescence on sparse tabular inputs: at the library-default threshold these neurons emitted no spikes and collapsed to the majority class. All other neuron families use the default threshold of 1.0.In order to minimize cross-entropy loss on the total output-layer spike counts, we train the networks using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10 −3 for 10 epochs and a batch size of 128 . The held-out validation split, a stratified 

90 /10 division of each training set, demonstrated convergence and plateaued well within the 10 -epoch budget on all datasets. In order to achieve complete reproducibility, we enable de-terministic cuDNN kernels and seed NumPy, PyTorch, and CUDA so that re-running the same configuration yields exact measurements. 540 trained models are produced by repeating each of the 9 × 3 × 4 = 108 (neuron, encoding, dataset) configurations over five seeds {42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 }. The stated inference latencies are mutually comparable because all runs use the same hardware and training loop and their absolute values are measured under shared GPU conditions, which should be interpreted as relative rather than absolute costs. A. Cross-Dataset Ranking 

Table II reports the five best (neuron, encoding) variants ranked by mean macro-F1 across the four datasets and five seeds, that is, 20 measurements per variant. The clearest observation is that all five top variants use latency encoding. Across the full sweep, the latency- and rate-encoded variants consistently outrank their delta-encoded counterparts, with delta occupying the lower tail of the 27 -variant ranking. This indicates that, for tabular intrusion features, the encoding choice is a stronger determinant of detection quality than the neuron model: within latency encoding the top five variants differ by less than one percentage point of macro-F1 and over-lap within a single standard deviation. The leading configura-tion, LeakyParallel/latency , attains 0.7998 ± 0.1989 

macro-F1, 0.9211 accuracy, 0.8677 MCC, and a low 0.0201 

false-alarm rate, yet is statistically indistinguishable from the other four latency variants. The relatively large standard deviations on macro-F1 reflect cross-dataset difficulty rather than seed instability: the same variant achieves near-perfect scores on CIC-IDS2017 and CTU-13 but substantially lower macro-F1 on NSL-KDD and KDDCup99, where the minority R2L and U2R classes (Table I) dominate the macro average. Per-seed variation, by contrast, is small, typically at or below 0.02 macro-F1, which confirms that the rankings are stable under reseeding. 

B. Quality and Inference Latency 

We look at classification quality (macro F1) and inference latency together since a high-quality detector has little practi-cal usefulness if it runs slowly. The per-dataset accuracy and inference latency for the top variants are shown in Table III along with a consistency score, which is the number of (dataset, metric) cells where a variant is ranked in the top three of this set. 

LeakyParallel/latency is the most consistent con-figuration, ranking in the top three on 7 of the 8 (dataset, metric) cells, missing only NSL-KDD accuracy, which is well above the level expected under random ranking. It attains the best accuracy on CIC-IDS2017 ( 0.9976 ) and CTU-13 (1.0000 ) and the lowest inference latency on CIC-IDS2017 (0.0730 ms per sample), with top-three latency on every dataset. The entire LeakyParallel family occupies the highest consistency scores, reflecting the speed advantage of its vectorized temporal unrolling. Because the latency-encoded variants already lead in detection quality (Table II) and LeakyParallel is the fastest neuron family among them, LeakyParallel/latency provides the strongest joint operating point; we therefore recommend it as a default for SNN-based intrusion detection when accuracy and infer-ence speed are both required.

Neuron choice is secondary for quality but relevant for inference cost. Within latency encoding, the nine neu-ron families differ by less than one point of macro-F1 (Table II) and their confidence intervals overlap, so they are effectively interchangeable in terms of detection quality. The practical decision, therefore, shifts to inference cost, where the choice matters: the LeakyParallel family is the fastest at inference on every dataset, owing to its vec-torized temporal unrolling. As the latency-encoded versions all win on quality, LeakyParallel/latency ranks very high on both detection quality and inference speed. We recommend using LeakyParallel/latency as the default operating point when detection quality and inference speed are equally important. 

Quality is not an artifact of imbalance. Observe that, in Table II, among the top variants, MCC exceed 0.86 , and macro recall, equivalently balanced accuracy, exceeds 0.78 .All these metrics are insensitive to a majority-class predictor, and therefore, the strong headline accuracy cannot be ex-plained by the networks simply predicting the dominant class. The corresponding detection rates at or above 0.88 and false-alarm rates at or below 0.02 further indicate that the latency-encoded detectors meet operationally relevant thresholds for intrusion detection. 

D. Threats to Validity 

Several factors bound the interpretation of our results. First, regarding fixed capacity, we hold the architecture ( T =25 ,

H=128 ) and training budget constant to ensure a fair neuron and encoding comparison; absolute scores could shift under larger networks or longer training, although the relative or-dering of encodings is unlikely to reverse given its consis-tency across four datasets. Second, regarding the evaluation protocol, NSL-KDD and KDDCup99 use their official splits, whereas CIC-IDS2017 and CTU-13 use a stratified random partition; the near-perfect, low-variance scores on CTU-13 in particular reflect both the discriminative power of our host-aggregated features and the fact that the same source hosts may appear in training and test under a stratified split. Scores under a stricter cross-scenario protocol with unseen botnet families would likely be lower, and we identify this as future work. Third, on class imbalance, we train on the natural class distribution (instead of using synthetic over-sampling), thus maintaining the comparability to the previous work, the realism for deployment, and not suffering from reduced recall on the minority classes of R2L and U2R (imbalance-aware training is an orthogonal extension). Fourth, in this study, our inference timing measurements are with an explicitly synchronized GPU. As a result, the relative comparisons are accurate but the absolute milliseconds are not readily applica-ble to dedicated or neuromorphic hardware. Fifth, regarding scope, this research isolates the SNN design space. A next step can be to compare against baselines under identical pre-processing, including non-spiking baselines, e.g., traditional models like random forests or deep neural network models. IV. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

In this study, we developed a controlled ablation of spik-ing neural networks for network intrusion detection. Nine 

snntorch neuron models were paired with three spike encoding schemes to create 27 variants, each of which was trained using five seeds and four benchmark datasets (a total of 540 models) under a single fixed pipeline. The report paints an accurate and coherent picture. Detection quality is primarily determined by the spike-encoding strategy rather than the neuron model: latency encoding leads throughout the sweep, rate encoding trails slightly, and delta encoding consistently comes last. The encoding advantage is most pronounced on datasets that contain enough class-separating signal. Within latency encoding the neuron families are sta-tistically interchangeable on quality, so the choice reduces to inference cost, where the LeakyParallel family is fastest due to its vectorized temporal unrolling. The result-ing configuration LeakyParallel/latency provides the best joint operating point, with an average 92 .11% accuracy, 

0.80 macro-F1, and 2.01% false-alarm rate across the four datasets, with near-perfect accuracy on CIC-IDS2017 and CTU-13 and top-three inference latency on every dataset. Robust agreement metrics for class imbalance, such as MCC 

∼ 0.87 confirm that this performance is not an artifact of class imbalance. Together, these results suggest that SNNs are a viable and feasible option for intrusion detection, especially in the low-latency and resource-constrained deployment context. Two research directions follow naturally from this work. First, because our threat model assumes a non-adaptive adver-sary, evaluating LeakyParallel/latency under adver-sarial evasion and data-poisoning attacks is a necessary step toward deployment. This is especially important for SNNs. Their non-differentiable spiking activations might provide some resistance to gradient-based changes. However, it is still unclear if this resistance holds up against adaptive, encoding-aware attacks or if it needs specific defenses like adversarial training. Second, LeakyParallel/latency 

is the strongest single setup. A hybrid design that centers on it could combine a fast spiking core with traditional or attention-based elements to boost feature discrimination. In the case of rare attack types on the communication channel, this is more applicable for network intrusion and attack detection, where the pure SNN struggles most.
