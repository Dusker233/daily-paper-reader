A B S T R A C T 

Organizational cybersecurity policies are often examined to determine whether they adequately comply standard security controls. This task is difficult because control statements are abstract, whereas policy documents describe governance practices in varied natural language. As a result, policy-based control assessment is time-consuming, difficult to standardize, and often difficult to document in a traceable manner. To address this gap, we present PROPARAG, an audit support approach for evaluating organizational cybersecurity policies against security controls autonomously. For each control, the approach retrieves relevant policy evidence, assesses coverage, identifies missing elements, and generates supporting explanations and recommendations. We evaluate PROPARAG on two real-world organizational policy corpora using 1,007 NIST SP 800-53 controls across both closed-source and open-source large language models (LLMs). The framework achieves F1 scores of 88.54 on OrgA and 82.31 on OrgB. The evaluation also shows that PROPARAG identifies relevant gaps in documented organizational policies and generates grounded recommendations for each identified gap. This research provides foundation for LLM-powered autonomous control-level assessment of organizational cybersecurity policies.

Relevant policy evidence may be distributed across multiple documents, expressed using different termi-nology, or only partially specified Sekwenz et al. (2025); Cram et al. (2017). ## 2. Problem Formulation 

We define the task as control-level assessment of organi-zational security policy corpora. For a given security control and an organizational policy corpus, the goal is to determine whether the organizational policy complies with the standard control objectives. The assessment focuses on documented policy coverage and does not validate operational imple-mentation, employee practice, system configuration, logs, tickets, interviews, or other forms of audit evidence. Each security control is assessed independently against the policy corpus. For each control, the assessment should identify relevant policy excerpts, assign a coverage label, identify missing policy elements when needed, and provide a short explanation that links the decision to the evidence. This control-level view supports audit review by making each decision traceable to specific policy evidence rather than only reporting an overall compliance judgment. 

2.1. Coverage Labels 

We use three coverage labels: FULLY_COVERED ,

> PARTIALLY_COVERED

, and NOT_COVERED . These labels describe how much support the policy corpus provides for agiven control. Table 1 summarizes the labels and their assessment criteria. The distinction between FULLY_COVERED 

and PARTIALLY_COVERED is important. A policy should not be treated as FULLY_COVERED only because it mentions a security topic. Full coverage requires detail to show how the control objective is addressed. 

2.2. Evidence and Gap Criteria 

Policy evidence is a policy excerpt that supports a coverage decision for a given control. It must be relevant to the control objective, useful for deciding the coverage label, and drawn from the policy corpus under assessment. External knowledge or general security best practices are not treated as evidence unless they are explicitly stated in the policy documents. A gap refers to a missing or weak policy element that prevents full coverage of acontrol. These gaps explain why the available evidence is incomplete and indicate what policy revision may be needed. ## 3. Methodology 

This section describes the overall PROPARAG frame-work. Figure 1 presents the workflow, followed by detailed descriptions of each component. The proposed framework is based on an agentic design, where LLM-based modules are placed at specific decision points to guide control-level assessment within the pipeline. Each module is discussed in detail in the following subsections. 

3.1. Policy Preprocessing 

PROPARAG begins by preparing each policy document for analysis. The document is converted into plain text, and formatting artifacts such as headers, footers, page numbers, and broken line breaks are removed. At the same time, important structural elements such as section headings, lists, tables, and appendices are retained to preserve the organi-zation of the content. The cleaned text is then divided into fixed-size chunks of 512 tokens, with an overlap of 50 tokens to maintain context across boundaries Saha et al. (2025a). Each chunk is stored along with metadata, including the doc-ument name, section heading (if available), page or source location, and a chunk index. Finally, these chunks are con-verted into vector representations using the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 

embedding model Reimers and Gurevych (2019) and stored in the Policy Corpus for use in this automated audit process. 

3.2. Control Knowledge Base and Control Representation 

The control knowledge base in PROPARAG stores stan-dardized security controls from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (e.g., NIST SP 800-53). It in-cludes key details such as the control identifier, description, control family, and assessment criteria required for evalu-ation. For each control, PROPARAG maintains a concise representation of its objective along with the type of evi-dence needed to satisfy it. For example, an access review control requires details on who performs the review, how often it occurs, which accounts are covered, how exceptions are handled, and how results are recorded. Similarly, an incident response control focuses on reporting channels, escalation roles, communication responsibilities, and post-incident review. This representation helps the system match policy content based on intent rather than exact wording. 

3.3. Evidence Retrieval 

For each control from control knowledgebase ,PROPARAG retrieves candidate policy segments from the policy corpus. These segments serve as evidence for evaluating control compliance. The retrieval module returns the relevant policy excerpts along with their metadata, including document source and location. This enables the final assessment to trace each decision back to its origin and supports auditor verification. 

: Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 3 of 19 3.4. Coverage Assessment 

The coverage assessment module determines whether the retrieved evidence addresses the control objective. It receives the control representation and retrieved policy evidence and assigns one of three labels: FULLY_COVERED ,

> PARTIALLY_COVERED

, or NOT_COVERED . A policy may not use the same terms as the control framework, but it can still express the same governance requirement. This requires reasoning-based models, such as LLMs, to accurately interpret seman-tic alignment during coverage assessment. The LLMs are prompted to follow a conservative de-cision. A control is marked as FULLY_COVERED only when the policy evidence clearly addresses the control objective with detail for audit review. If the policy discusses the topic but omits important details, the control is marked as 

> PARTIALLY_COVERED

. If no sufficient evidence is found, the control is marked as NOT_COVERED . The prompt used for cover-age assessment is listed in Appendix B.1 for reproducibility. 

3.5. Gap Analysis and Recommendations 

When the coverage decision is PARTIALLY_COVERED or 

> NOT_COVERED

, the orchestration agent calls the gap analysis module. This module identifies missing or weak policy elements that prevent the control from being fully cov-ered. Common gaps include missing ownership, missing procedure, weak enforcement language, absent review cy-cle, limited scope, unclear exception handling, and missing documentation requirements. The identified gaps are linked to the retrieved evidence. This prevents the system from producing generic feedback that is not supported by the policy corpus. For example, if a policy states that privileged accounts must be reviewed but does not specify review frequency or responsible authority, the system may identify missing review cycle and missing ownership as gaps. The prompt used for gap analysis is listed in Appendix B.2. The recommendation module then generates policy-level guidance based on the identified gaps. These recommenda-tions describe what should be added, clarified, or revised in the policy. They remain at the governance-policy level and do not prescribe technical implementation details unless such details are directly required by the control. The prompt used for recommendation is listed in Appendix B.3. 

3.6. Orchestration Module 

The orchestration agent acts as the central controller of PROPARAG. It maintains the assessment state for each control, including the control representation, retrieved ev-idence, coverage label, identified gaps, recommendations, and explanations. Based on this state, it determines which module to invoke at each step. The workflow is conditional rather than fixed. After retrieving evidence, the orchestration agent forwards it to the coverage assessment module. If the control is marked as FULLY_COVERED , the process can stop without further analysis. If the evidence is insufficient, the agent proceeds to gap analysis and then generates policy-level recommendations. 3.7. Explainability and Traceability 

After coverage assessment and any required gap anal-ysis, PROPARAG generates an evidence-linked explana-tion. The explanation connects the control objective, the retrieved policy evidence, the assigned coverage label, and the identified gaps. This step is important because audit review requires understanding about the decision of the model on coverage. The auditors should be able to see why a label was assigned and which policy excerpts support that decision. Therefore model is prompted to produce the concise and evidence-focused explanation. The prompt used for this module is describe in Appendix B.4. 

3.8. Report Generation 

For each control, PROPARAG produces a structured assessment record. Table 2 summarizes the fields included in this record. The report generation module aggregates these control-level records into an audit summary. The summary reports the number of controls in each coverage class, high-lights recurring policy gaps, and can group results by control family. This helps reviewers identify areas where policy coverage is stronger or weaker. The prompt used for report generation is listed in Appendix B.5.

4.3. Systems Compared 

This section describes the systems considered in our evaluation. We first present the full PROPARAG pipeline, followed by a set of baseline systems used for comparison. 

4.3.1. PROPARAG (Full Pipeline) 

Our full system includes: (1) intent-conditioned seman-tic retrieval, (2) decomposed compliance reasoning (cover-age → gaps → recommendations → explanation), and (3) evidence-linked justification. We instantiate PROPARAG using multiple backbone LLMs, to assess robustness across model families. 

4.3.2. Baselines 

To evaluate the effectiveness of PROPARAG, we com-pare it with a set of representative baselines that reflect com-mon design choices in LLM-based policy analysis. These include variations in retrieval strategies, reasoning structure, and the level of analysis, as described below. 

B1: Single-Shot LLM (No Retrieval). The model re-ceives the control text and policy corpus and directly predicts the coverage label in a single step without explicit evidence retrieval. 

B2: Semantic Retrieval + Labeling (No Decomposi-tion). We retain the retrieval stage, but replace decomposed reasoning with a single prompt that jointly performs cov-erage prediction, gap identification, and recommendation generation. 

B3: Lexical Retrieval (BM25) + Labeling. We replace semantic retrieval with BM25 retrieval using the raw control text as the query, followed by single-step coverage predic-tion. 

B4: Standard Dense Retrieval (RAG) + Labeling. We use embedding-based retrieval with the control text as the query, followed by a re-ranker and a single-step coverage prediction. 

B5: Document-Level Retrieval Baseline. Instead of re-trieving fine-grained segments, we retrieve whole docu-ments and perform coverage prediction. We note that traditional rule-based or keyword matching approaches are not directly comparable in this setting, as they lack the ability to perform semantic alignment between abstract control specifications and natural-language policy text.

4.4. Evaluation Metrics 

Coverage Analysis. We evaluate control-level predictions against human ground truth using standard multi-class clas-sification metrics, including overall accuracy, precision, re-call, and F1-score. Confusion matrices are additionally re-ported to analyze misclassification patterns across coverage categories. We further assess statistical significance using McNemar’s test to evaluate differences in classification per-formance between methods. 

Evidence Quality. To assess retrieval effectiveness, we evaluate the quality of policy excerpts selected as evidence for control assessments. The stratified subset consists of 

200 controls per organization, balanced across coverage categories. Retrieval precision at rank 𝑘 is computed as: Precision@k = Number of relevant excerpts in top-𝑘 𝑘 (1) Relevance is determined based on whether the retrieved excerpt contains sufficient information to support coverage assessment according to the annotation rubric. We also report the Top-1 sufficiency rate, defined as the proportion of controls for which the highest-ranked excerpt alone is deemed sufficient for coverage assessment. 

Diagnostic and Recommendation Utility. For controls labeled PARTIALLY_COVERED or NOT_COVERED , human auditors evaluate the usefulness of structured diagnostics. Specifi-cally, they assess whether identified gaps correctly reflect missing control elements (gap correctness), whether gen-erated recommendations are actionable and aligned with control intent (recommendation usefulness), and whether explanations clearly link conclusions to policy evidence (explanation clarity). These dimensions are evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale by expert annotators and summarized using mean agreement scores across evaluated controls. 

Efficiency. Finally, we measure deployment feasibility by reporting average tokens consumed per control evaluation, average latency per control, and total monetary cost per full corpus assessment. These efficiency metrics quantify com-putational overhead and practical scalability of the frame-work in real-world audit settings. These metrics provide insight into the trade-off between reasoning depth and computational efficiency in practical audit settings. 

4.5. Implementation Details 

This subsection presents a brief summary of implemen-tation details relevant to reproducibility and experimental consistency. These details are provided to clarify experimen-tal conditions rather than to describe system engineering choices. All models are evaluated in a zero-shot setting without task-specific fine-tuning. Prompt templates are fixed across all experiments to ensure that observed differences arise from methodological design choices rather than prompt variation. The prompts used for each instance are provided in Appendix B. Control representations, including control intent and expected policy elements, are generated once per control and reused consistently across experimental runs. To mitigate variability introduced by stochastic text generation, we use fixed generation parameters for deterministic runs (temperature = 0, top-p = 1). No ground-truth labels are exposed to the models during inference. All metrics are computed post hoc by comparing model outputs against the independently constructed expert annotations. All metrics are computed directly from model outputs and retrieved policy evidence, consistent with the decision-support scope. These implementation choices are intended to support fair comparison across methods while avoiding optimization for any specific model or configura-tion. 

Experimental Protocol. For each policy document, com-pliance assessment is performed on a per-control basis. This protocol produces a control-specific assessment consisting of a coverage state, diagnostic gaps when applicable, and evidence-linked explanations. No information from other controls is provided during evaluation, ensuring that each as-sessment is performed independently without cross-control leakage. 

Models Evaluated. We evaluate the methodology us-ing eight models, comprising four closed-source and four open-source large language models (LLMs). Closed-source models represent commercially available systems accessed through hosted APIs, while open-source models are de-ployed locally. The benchmark includes four closed-source models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Claude 3.5 Haiku) and four open-source models (LLaMA3.1 7B, Mistral 7B, Qwen2.5 7B, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B). Model prompts and reasoning stages are kept consistent across evaluations to isolate the effects of method-ological design choices rather than prompt engineering dif-ferences.

Notably, performance differences between OrgA and OrgB stem from variations in policy structure and the level of explanatory detail, which directly influence evidence alignment and coverage assessment. Despite these differ-ences, PROPARAG exhibits strong generalization across heterogeneous policy articulation styles. Importantly, while absolute performance varies across backbone models, the relative ranking of models remains stable across both organi-zations, suggesting that the observed trends reflect intrinsic model capabilities rather than dataset-specific effects. 

5.1.2. Baseline Comparison

Across both organizations, PROPARAG achieves the highest overall performance in terms of accuracy and F1. These improvements are consistent across precision and recall, indicating that gains are driven by method. The single-shot baseline (B1) performs substantially worse on both corpora, highlighting the limitations of con-trol coverage assessment without explicit evidence retrieval. In the absence of grounding, predictions are more suscep-tible to ambiguity in policy language, leading to reduced precision and unstable recall. Introducing retrieval (B2 and B3) results in clear perfor-mance improvements over single-shot prompting. The document-level baseline (B5) underperforms rela-tive to control-centric methods, reinforcing the importance of decomposing compliance evaluation at the control level rather than performing monolithic policy classification. Overall, the results demonstrate that while retrieval sig-nificantly improves control-level prediction, structured con-trol decomposition and staged, intent-conditioned reasoning in PROPARAG provide substantial additional gains beyond conventional RAG-based pipelines.

The magnitude of improvement suggests that access to structured evidence is a primary driver of performance in compliance evaluation tasks. However, retrieval alone remains insufficient to reach the performance of the full PROPARAG pipeline, highlighting the complementary role of staged, control-centric reasoning.

## 7. Discussion 

7.1. Methodological Effectiveness 

Across both organizational corpora, PROPARAG con-sistently outperforms all evaluated baselines in control-level coverage prediction. Compared to the strongest baseline (Dense RAG + Labeling), PROPARAG achieves substan-tial improvements in overall accuracy and F1, with the largest gains observed on the more structurally complex OrgB corpus. These improvements are not limited to aggre-gate metrics. The retrieval ablation (Section 5.2) confirms that intent-conditioned evidence grounding meaningfully in-creases sensitivity to missing policy elements. Importantly, performance remains stable across diverse backbone models with consistent patterns. This indicates that the majority of performance gains arise from methodological structure. 

7.2. Implications for Audit Workflows 

Our findings reveal several implications for audit work-flows. In particular, PROPARAG exhibits three key proper-ties that are especially relevant in practice. First, the system demonstrates conservative judg-ment behavior. Open-source backbones tend to prefer

in ambiguous cases, while closed mod-els more decisively identify explicit gaps. This calibration pattern suggests that the pipeline avoids over-asserting full compliance in the absence of explicit evidence.

Second, structured gap modeling increases visibility into specific governance deficiencies. Rather than producing bi-nary compliance outcomes, the system decomposes defi-ciencies into interpretable categories (e.g., missing respon-sibility, absent enforcement, insufficient procedural detail), which aligns with real-world audit reporting practices. Third, explanations are consistently grounded in re-trieved policy excerpts. Human evaluation confirms high groundedness scores, indicating that diagnostic outputs are traceable to explicit evidence rather than speculative rea-soning. This traceability enhances audit transparency and reduces the risk of hallucinated justifications. Collectively, these findings suggest that PROPARAG is a structured compliance reasoning framework that supports auditor-aligned, evidence-grounded, and operationally inter-pretable policy evaluation. 

7.3. Limitations and Future Directions 

Despite strong overall performance, a few systematic challenges remain and point to important directions for future work. First, PROPARAG’s effectiveness is influenced by the quality and structure of input policies. Variations in pol-icy articulation, such as differences in granularity, implicit assumptions, or lack of standardized formatting, can im-pact evidence retrieval and coverage assessment. While the framework generalizes across organizations, improving ro-bustness to poorly structured or implicitly defined policies remains an open challenge. Second, the PARTIALLY_COVERED class continues to present inherent ambiguity. Determining partial compliance often requires nuanced interpretation of incomplete or distributed evidence across multiple policy sections. This can lead to boundary uncertainty, even for stronger models, and sug-gests the need for more refined reasoning strategies or hi-erarchical aggregation mechanisms. Third, although expert evaluation confirms strong evi-dence grounding and interpretability, human evaluation re-mains limited in scale. Expanding expert-in-the-loop vali-dation across diverse domains and regulatory frameworks would further strengthen the generalizability and reliability of the system. Finally, future work can extend PROPARAG toward more comprehensive compliance settings, including multi-document reasoning, cross-policy dependency analysis, and alignment with multiple regulatory standards. Integrating uncertainty estimation and confidence calibration into the reasoning pipeline may also improve trustworthiness in high-stakes audit scenarios. 

## 8. Related Work 

Early work on policy compliance relied on manual audits and checklist-based review processes, which require signifi-cant human effort and are difficult to scale. Initial compu-tational approaches used rule-based systems and keyword matching to align policy text with control requirements, but these methods lack semantic understanding and often fail to capture implicit policy coverage. Another line of work focuses on policy enforcement and governance in AI systems. Madan Madan (2025) proposes ArGen, which enforces compliance through policy-as-code and optimization-based alignment. These approaches focus on enforcement, rule gen-eration, or agent behavior rather than evaluating existing organizational policies. ## 9. Conclusion 

This work presents security policy compliance assess-ment as a control-centric semantic reasoning problem and demonstrates how structured decomposition improves au-dit workflows. By enabling control-level evaluation with staged reasoning, PROPARAG provides a more transparent and traceable alternative to document-centric approaches. Through systematic evaluation on real-world policy corpora, we show that retrieval design and reasoning structure sig-nificantly influence coverage prediction. This also affects diagnostic clarity and explanation groundedness. From a governance and decision-support perspective, these findings position PROPARAG as a more systematic, explainable, and audit-ready approach to compliance assessment. In practice, the framework streamlines audit workflows, reduces manual effort, and improves consistency in evaluating complex pol-icy documents. Overall, this work highlights the importance of reasoning-centric design for policy analysis. It further es-tablishes structured, control-level compliance assessment as a principled foundation for reliable, auditor-aligned systems supporting autonomous governance, auditing, and regula-tory processes.

## A. Coverage Annotation Rubric 

This appendix formalizes the rubric used for expert ground truth construction. Annotations were performed by domain experts with prior experience in cybersecurity gov-ernance and policy auditing. Each control was evaluated based on the presence, specificity, and enforceability of aligned policy evidence. 1. Fully Covered 

• Explicit articulation of control objective 

• Clearly defined scope and applicability 

• Assigned responsibility or ownership 

• Defined enforcement, oversight, or review mechanism 

• Procedural or operational guidance supporting implementation 2. Partially Covered 

• Implicit or incomplete articulation of control intent 

• Missing or ambiguously defined responsibility assignment 

• Lacking enforcement or review specification 

• Insufficient procedural clarity 

• Coverage limited to high-level policy statements 3. Not Covered 

• No substantive policy text aligned with control intent 

• References present but unrelated to control re-quirements In cases of ambiguity, annotators adopted a conservative interpretation, defaulting to Partially Covered unless explicit and enforceable policy evidence was present. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and adjudication to ensure labeling consistency. 

## B. Prompt Templates 

The following prompt templates operationalize the rea-soning stages of PROPARAG. Each prompt is role-specific and enforces structured outputs to ensure reproducibility, au-dit traceability, and consistent coverage classification across backbone models. 

B.1. Coverage Judgment 

Purpose: Determine the degree of compliance of a se-curity control and assign a corresponding level of coverage. This prompt evaluates whether the retrieved policy evidence sufficiently satisfies a given security control. The model is instructed to apply strict, evidence-grounded reasoning and classify the control into one of three mutually exclusive cate-gories: FULLY_COVERED, PARTIALLY_COVERED, or NOT_COVERED. The structured output format enforces consistent status labeling, confidence estimation, and ex-plicit justification. 

B.2. Gap Detection 

Purpose: Identify specific policy-level compliance gaps This prompt analyzes controls labeled PARTIALLY_COVERED or 

NOT_COVERED and extracts concrete deficiencies in the policy text. The agent is instructed to identify missing elements, in-complete governance provisions, or ambiguities that prevent full compliance. Outputs are structured to isolate actionable policy-level deficiencies rather than technical implementa-tion details.
