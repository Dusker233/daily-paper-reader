# Abstract 

A central challenge in large-scale decision-making under incomplete information is estimat-ing reliable probabilities. Recent approaches use Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate ex-planatory factors and coarse-grained probability estimates, which are then refined by a Naïve Bayes model over factor combinations. However, sparse factor spaces often yield “unknown” pre-dictions, while expanding factors increases noise and spurious correlations, weakening conditional independence and degrading reliability. To ad-dress these limitations, we propose A NCHOR , an aggregated Bayesian inference framework over a hierarchical factor space. It constructs dense factor hierarchies through iterative generation and clustering, maps contexts via hierarchical retrieval and refinement, and augments Naïve Bayes with a Causal Bayesian Network to model latent fac-tor dependencies. Experiments show that A N- 

> CHOR

markedly reduces “unknown” predictions and produces more reliable probability estimates than direct LLM baselines, achieving state-of-the-art performance while significantly reducing time and token overhead.

# 2. Related Work 

Decomposition-Based Reasoning Like many advanced reasoning frameworks, A NCHOR decomposes complex problems into smaller components. This builds on a rich body of work, from explicitly breaking down questions into procedural steps (Wolfson et al., 2020; Press et al., 2023) or generating faithful reasoning chains (Tafjord et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023), to the widely used Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022). More recent struc-tured approaches aim for greater cognitive plausibility (Yao et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2023), maintain an explicit belief graph (Kassner et al., 2023), or model latent variables over the reasoning chain or themes (Hoffman et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). While related, the latent variables in A N-

> CHOR

’s Causal Bayesian Network represent abstract con-cepts rather than token sequences. The primary distinction of our method, however, is its proactive factor-space con-struction. Instead of decomposing reactively for each query, ANCHOR employs “bottom-up abduction” to build a compre-hensive, hierarchically structured factor space. This design mitigates the issue of factor sparsity, creating a persistent and reusable reasoning structure that reduces the generation of “unknown” predictions. 

Probabilistic Inference and Uncertainty Estimations 

Prior work estimates LLM uncertainty from intrinsic signals such as token probabilities (Luo et al., 2026b) or verbal-ized confidence (Xiong et al., 2023), which are often mis-calibrated, and also uses sampling-based methods (Kuhn et al., 2023) or Bayesian prompt inference (Derakhshani et al., 2023). Another line fine-tunes models to amortize Bayesian inference or automate parts of model specifica-tion (Hu et al., 2024), but these approaches can be unre-liable or computationally costly in practice. In contrast, ANCHOR performs calibration through explicit probabilistic modeling (Feng et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2024). Specifi-cally, it orchestrates a Naïve Bayes classifier (Zhang, 2004) and a latent-augmented CBN that models dependencies be-tween factors beyond NB’s conditional-independence as-sumption (Prabha et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025). Within this framework, the LLM supports both parameter elicita-tion (Nafar et al., 2025) and dynamic structure construction for the CBN (Babakov et al., 2025). # 3. Preliminaries 

3.1. Problem Formulation 

We formulate reliable decision-making as a contextual bi-nary inference task. For clarity, App. A.1 summarizes the notation used throughout the main text. Given a context 

C and two competing hypotheses, O1 and O2, the objec-tive is to estimate the conditional probabilities P (Oi | C)

for i ∈ { 1, 2}. A calibrated estimate allows a system to robustly decide which hypothesis is more plausible. Follow-ing Feng et al. (2025), we decompose C into a high-level scenario Scen and a downstream condition u, enabling a neu-tral reasoning space based on Scen that mitigates biases from 

u. For completeness, we treat an “unknown” prediction as abstention: letting F⋆ denote the factor–mapping oper-ator (see §4.2), the system yields an unknown prediction iff |F ⋆(u)| = 0 or max i∈{ 1,2} P (Oi | C) < τ for a preset 

τ ∈ (0 , 1) .

# 4. Methodology 

In this section, we introduce A NCHOR , a three-stage frame-work designed to transform raw LLM outputs into well-calibrated probabilities. As illustrated in Figure 2, the pro-cess consists of factor-space construction, context-aware mapping, and probabilistic inference. 

4.1. Iterative Abduction for Factor–Space Expansion 

4.1.1. B OTTOM -UP ABDUCTION STRATEGY 

We define bottom-up abduction as a two-stage process that inverts the traditional paradigm by decoupling factor gen-eration from structuring. First, we iteratively generate a comprehensive set of factors, and then we cluster and theme them to build a structured factor space. The generation stage begins with an empty set F(0) = ∅

and iterates until reaching a target size Ntarget or exceeding 

Tmax rounds: (a) Contextual sentence generation , where a few-shot prompt elicits b diverse supporting or refuting sentences per scenario, explicitly encouraging varied reason-ing chains and broad aspect coverage; and (b) Factor har-vesting and validation , where an LLM extraction prompt identifies all distinct factors in the sentences, which are parsed into a candidate set ∆F, validated for semantic non-redundancy and domain relevance, and merged into the main set via F(t+1) = F(t) ∪ ∆F.Our factor-generation proce-dure achieves geometric convergence in recall completeness. 4.3. Probabilistic Inference with Elicited Parameters 

4.3.1. P ARAMETERIZATION AND INFERENCE 

The final stage of our framework transforms the mapped factor set, F∗(u), into a calibrated probability for the com-peting hypotheses, O1 and O2. To achieve this, we first define the structure of two probabilistic models—a Naïve Bayes (NB) model and a Causal Bayesian Network (CBN). We then describe the process of eliciting the necessary pa-rameters from an LLM and, finally, how these parameters are used to perform inference in each model. 

Naïve Bayes Model. The Naïve Bayes model is structured on the assumption that all factors are conditionally indepen-dent given the hypothesis. It consists of a root Outcome node (representing O1 and O2) with directed edges to each child factor node fj ∈ F ∗(u).

Latent-Augmented Causal Bayesian Network. To cap-ture dependencies between factors, we construct a CBN whose structure is learned dynamically for each scenario. We prompt an LLM to act as a causal discovery engine: given the list of relevant factors, it identifies a set of la-tent variables L = {L1, . . . , L k} and partitions the factors among them. Each latent variable Li ∈ { 0, 1} represents an abstract concept that mediates correlations among the factors assigned to it. Accordingly, we add directed edges Outcome → Li and Li → fj for each factor fj grouped under Li. Outcomes serve as roots and the latents are in-termediate parents shared by factors, relaxing NB’s inde-pendence assumption by making factors independent only when conditioned on their latent parent. Inspired by prior work validating the use of LLMs to elicit informative priors (Riegler et al., 2025; Nafar et al., 2025; Capstick et al., 2025), our framework efficiently parameter-izes its Bayesian networks. We directly query the LLM to obtain the necessary conditional probabilities—both at the factor and latent levels—thus bypassing the need for costly data sampling. 

Factor-Level Parameters : For each factor f , we elicit its posterior probability given hypothesis O1, denoted as 

ϕf = P (O1 | f ). This captures the evidential strength of a single factor in favor of O1. For the Naïve Bayes (NB) model, which is parameterized by likelihoods P (f | Ok),we adopt a simple binary, symmetric-prior approximation and set θf = P (f | O1) ≈ ϕf , P (f | O2) ≈ 1 − ϕf .

Latent-Level Parameters : Exclusively for the Causal Bayesian Network (CBN), we elicit the conditional proba-bility of each latent variable Li given a hypothesis, such as 

P (Li = 1 | O1) and P (Li = 0 | O2). To ensure numerical stability, all elicited probabilities are smoothed. These pa-rameters are then used to build and perform inference in our two probabilistic models. 

Evidence representation. We treat each factor f ∈F∗(u) as a binary indicator, where f = 1 denotes that the factor is active for u. Let E ⊆ F ∗(u) denote the set of observed active factors. For simplicity, factors not in E are treated as unobserved and are omitted from the likelihood in both NB and CBN. 5.1. Experimental Setups Datasets We evaluate our model on three reasoning and planning benchmarks from Feng et al. (2025), each formatted as a decision - support tuple (Scen , U, O ): To-day (1,000 instances), Plasma (279 scenarios, 1,395 in-stances) and Common2Sense (216 scenarios, 3,822 in-stances), the Common2Sense’s test split being manu-ally expanded under an unbiased protocol to boost di-versity. To assess decision - making beyond planning, we further sample 100 examples from each of the four fact-checking datasets— XSum (Tang et al., 2024), CNN (Tang et al., 2024), ExpertQA (Malaviya et al., 2024) and 

COVID (Saakyan et al., 2021)—treating each document as condition U and each claim as outcome O, with Scen left empty. (dataset details refer to B.3). 

Settings We conduct all experiments with four LLMs: Qwen2.5 - 32B, Qwen2.5 - 72B (Bai et al., 2023), DeepSeekV3 - 671B (DeepSeek-AI, 2024), and GPT-4 (Ope-nAI, 2023). The Qwen models were deployed locally, while DeepSeekV3 - 671B was accessed through DeepSeek’s official API. We set K1 = 3 and K2 = 5 in the KNN search; more hyperparameters and settings are provided in Appendix B.4. All experiments run on 4× NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24GB) GPUs. 

Preference-based Pairwise Evaluation To begin, we evaluate our model on Common2Sense using the preference-based pairwise evaluation framework introduced by Feng et al. (2025). The experimental setup is as follows. Given a scenario Scen and two potential outcomes, O1 and O2, we se-lect two distinct additional conditions, U1 and U2. Crucially, both conditions are constructed to support the same outcome, 

O1, over O2. These are then used to form two slightly dif-ferent contexts: C1 = Scen + U1 and C2 = Scen + U2. The model’s task is to assign conditional probabilities P (Oi|C1)

and P (Oi|C2) for each outcome i ∈ { 1, 2}. A successful evaluation requires the model to correctly discern the subtle difference in the degree of support between the two con-texts. For instance, if human judgment determines that C1

provides stronger support for O1 than C2 does, we expect the model’s probability assignments to satisfy the follow-ing relationship: P (O1|C1) > P (O1|C2) > P (O2|C2) >P (O2|C1), we set the abstention threshold τ = 0 , so the system yields an unknown prediction only when no factors are matched, i.e., |F ⋆(u)| = 0 .

Decision Making Evaluation We evaluate A NCHOR ’s decision-making on reasoning and planning datasets (Plasma, Today) and four fact-checking datasets for gen-eralization (ExpertQA, XSum, COVID, CNN). In each task, given a context C and two outcomes O1 and O2, the model must select the more plausible one. A decision is consid-ered correct if the chosen outcome has a higher conditional probability, e.g., satisfying P (O1|C) > P (O2|C) when O1

is the ground truth. 

Baseline Methods We compare our approach against several baselines for probability estimation from LLMs in our experiments: (1) BIRD : Builds a structured fac-tor space via single - pass abductive reasoning and uses a Naïve Bayes model for inference (Feng et al., 2025). (2) 

Vanilla : Prompts the model to verbalize its estimated prob-ability (Wang et al., 2023). (3) Logits : Converts the normal-ized token probability of the decision token into a probability score. (4) CoT : Elicits a chain - of -thought reasoning process before asking for the final probability (Wei et al., 2022). (5) 

Compare : Selects which of two conditions better supports the outcome without producing individual probabilities, an unfair setting for methods relying on those estimates (Feng et al., 2025). (6) Factor - based : Averages probabilities over five factors generated with knowledge of the gold outcome, making direct comparison invalid.

For decision-making, following Feng et al. (2025), when the B IRD baseline predicts “unknown”, we default to the CoT method to ensure a decision is made. As other methods do not predict “unknown”, we omit the coverage metric. ExpertQA, XSum, CNN, and Today represent more chal-lenging settings due to long contexts or temporal reasoning, while COVID and Plasma are relatively simpler (Feng et al., 2025). For decision -making on fact - checking datasets, we convert probabilistic outputs into support labels by apply-ing a fixed threshold, following prior work in fact verifica-tion (Jayaweera et al., 2024). For the Plasma and Today datasets, we adhere to the evaluation of (Feng et al., 2025). As shown in Table 2, A NCHOR consistently outperforms all baselines at the 72B scale and even surpasses the 671B model on the harder tasks.

# 6. Conclusion 

We introduced the A NCHOR framework to generate reliable and calibrated probability estimates from Large Language Models for critical decision-making. By integrating a hier-archical factor-space construction with the orchestration of Naïve Bayes and a Causal Bayesian Network, our frame-work showed notable enhancements in decision-making accuracy and alignment with human judgment. Our exper-imental results underscore the effectiveness of A NCHOR ,thereby increasing the practical utility of LLMs in high-stakes scenarios and fostering more trustworthy autonomous systems. 

# Impact Statement 

This work improves decision support for contextual binary inference by combining LLM-generated reasoning factors with explicit Bayesian inference, producing more calibrated probabilities and a traceable audit trail while reducing ab-stentions ( unknown ) and lowering inference time and token cost. However, because the factor hierarchy, latent grouping, and elicited parameters depend on LLM behavior, biases or hallucinated structures may be propagated into the re-sulting Bayesian models, and reducing unknown can trade abstention for confident but incorrect factor mappings under distribution shift or adversarial inputs. We therefore posi-tion A NCHOR as a decision-support tool rather than a fully automated decision-maker, and recommend human over-sight, conservative abstention thresholds, and routine audits of mapped factors and inferred structures, with robustness, fairness, and adversarial resilience treated as prerequisites for high-stakes deployment.

A.5. Limitations and Future Work Dataset and evaluation. Our dataset is constructed from scenario-local pairwise comparisons: each scenario contains exactly two opposing statements (one is gold) and a pool of supporting sentences, and annotations are collected by comparing sampled sentence pairs within the same scenario. This yields many small, weakly connected comparison subgraphs rather than a single dense pairwise graph. Consequently, global ranking models such as Bradley–Terry would largely reduce to independent two-way fits and provide limited additional insight beyond instance-level metrics. For similar reasons, ECE-style calibration errors are not ideal in this setting: confidence-bin estimates can be noisy due to the lack of globally comparable, repeatedly evaluated items and limited per-bin support. These are dataset-induced limitations. Future work can migrate the task and method to datasets with denser, better connected comparisons or globally comparable labels, where BT-style ranking and calibration evaluation become more informative. 

Reliance on LLM-provided probabilities. Although our approach only asks the LLM for coarse relative probability rankings to initialize CPT parameters, it still relies on LLM-generated probability judgments. This dependence may inherit miscalibration or prompt sensitivity from the underlying model, and it remains unclear how best to elicit, regularize, or replace such probabilistic inputs. A promising direction for future work is to study more robust probability acquisition and grounding mechanisms, such as anchoring CPT initialization with empirical frequencies, lightweight calibration on held-out data, or alternative elicitation objectives that reduce sensitivity while preserving the usefulness of relative rankings.

Additional Details for the B IRD -on-A NCHOR Ablations Complete-information spaces in B IRD vs. A NCHOR . Conceptually, B IRD assumes that each factor takes two mutually exclusive attribute values. For a given scenario, these categorical factors form a low-arity product space, so the complete-information training set can be obtained by (conceptually) enumerating this Cartesian product and sampling from it. In ANCHOR , factors are standalone evidence-like statements drawn from a global pool and organized into clusters and themes, not tied to a fixed two-value choice per slot. A complete-information assignment is thus a subset of this pool, and naively forming a Cartesian product over clusters would both generate many incoherent combinations and lead to a combinatorial explosion. 

Structured Monte Carlo scheme for CPT training. To plug B IRD ’s CPT-based outcome model into A NCHOR ’s factor space, we therefore adopt a structured Monte Carlo scheme. C.2. Detailed Algorithm Specifications 

This section provides the detailed pseudo-code for the three core stages of the A NCHOR framework, as described in Section 4. Algorithm 1 details the construction of the hierarchical factor space. It operates by iteratively generating raw factors, validating them via self-consistency voting, and finally organizing them using a pipeline of embedding, clustering, and LLM-based thematic pruning. Algorithm 2 specifies the multi-stage process for mapping a condition to a relevant factor set. The pipeline begins with a broad, two-level hierarchical retrieval to generate candidates, followed by a voting-based filtering step and a final reflective refinement to ensure high precision. Algorithm 3 outlines the procedure for transforming a set of mapped factors into a final probability. It involves parameterizing two parallel models—Naïve Bayes and a latent-augmented Causal Bayesian Network—with probabilities elicited from an LLM, and then aggregating their posteriors for a robust final estimate.
